# Highlights

- Flexibility programmes need load intervals reliable per building and per week
- Pooled conformal intervals leave many newly enrolled buildings under-covered
- Two-timescale calibration: population prior plus 7-day per-building feedback
- Weekly under-coverage falls below a static-calibration oracle reference
- Weekly-tuned feedback gives most of the gain; the prior adds early reliability

# Abstract

Flexibility and demand-response programmes check the performance of each enrolled building in each settlement period, so day-ahead load intervals must be reliable building by building and week by week, from the first days after metering starts. Conformal intervals pooled over other buildings are calibrated only on average. We propose two-timescale shrinkage conformal calibration (TTSC): a slow component shrinks the score distribution of previously onboarded buildings towards the new building's recency-weighted own scores, and a fast component corrects the building's miscoverage level from its misses in the last seven days. Both parameters are selected by leave-one-building-out for weekly reliability. On 1,042 buildings (Building Data Genome 2) and 316 Portuguese electricity clients, compared with pooled, online, temporal, hierarchical and shrinkage calibration on the same forecasts, pooled conformalised quantile regression left 23.7% and 18.4% of new buildings under-covered in a typical week. TTSC reduced this to 13.6% and 6.7%, below a static-calibration oracle reference, and buildings with any week below 0.80 from 51.2% to 20.0% (BDG2), at a median interval score 3–4 % higher. Feedback tuned for weekly reliability provided most of the weekly gain; the shrinkage prior kept window-level and early under-coverage low. Results held on runs held out from the method's design, unseen sites, cross-country transfer and fourteen stress tests. In stylised weekly-settled capacity-headroom offers, TTSC reduced failed weekly checks from 17.5% to 14.1% of building-weeks at unchanged average cost.

**Keywords:** probabilistic load forecasting; conformal prediction; cold start; demand response; building energy flexibility; adaptive calibration


# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background and motivation

Buildings account for a large share of electricity demand, and their flexible loads are increasingly enrolled in demand response, flexibility markets and on-site renewable integration [1]. These applications act on day-ahead statements of the form "tomorrow's hourly load of *this* building will lie between $L$ and $U$ with probability $1-\alpha$". Demand-response baselines and their settlement, day-ahead capacity and flexibility offers, measurement and verification [2] and anomaly alarms all depend on such intervals. Probabilistic load forecasting has therefore become a central topic in energy forecasting [3,4], and recent studies in this journal have developed expressive probabilistic models for loads, renewables and multi-energy systems [5–7].

Intervals are needed most when a building has just been connected to a monitoring, aggregation or demand-response platform, which is exactly when the least building-specific data exist. Aggregators onboard buildings continuously, and programme performance is checked per building and per settlement period: a building whose upper bound is exceeded far more often than stated in a given week exposes the operator to penalties, failed performance checks or false alarms for that building and that week, however well the intervals perform on average over the portfolio and the season. Three properties are therefore required at once. The intervals must be reliable *building by building*, *week by week*, and *from the first days* after metering starts, without months of building-specific history. This study asks how close day-ahead load intervals can come to these requirements, and how.

## 1.2 Related work

*Forecasting with little building-specific data.* Data-driven building forecasting traditionally needs months to years of history per building [8]. Transfer learning, federated learning and global or pretrained models now provide useful point forecasts for buildings with little history [9–13], and large pretrained models report zero-shot probabilistic forecasts for unseen buildings [14]. A recent cross-building study found that split-conformal intervals under-cover unseen buildings on average and recommended recalibration before deployment [15]. These studies evaluate point accuracy or *pooled* interval metrics; whether the intervals are reliable for *each* new building, and from which day on, has not been examined.

*Conformal calibration in energy forecasting.* Conformal prediction gives distribution-free intervals around any forecaster [16–18], with conformalised quantile regression (CQR) as a strong default [19]. It is increasingly used for loads, prices and renewables, for example in day-ahead and intraday power markets [20], with adaptive conformal inference (ACI) [21], local online conformal prediction for power-load intervals [22] and context-aware calibration for renewable forecasts [23]. Its guarantee is *marginal*: averaged over exchangeable test points. For a fleet of new buildings, a marginal guarantee is compatible with intervals that are much too narrow for volatile buildings and too wide for regular ones, while pooled coverage looks perfect. An early comparison of interval methods across load profiles already observed that reliability differs between profiles [24].

*Unit-level and temporal calibration.* The statistics literature offers several remedies, each developed under different assumptions. Group-wise (Mondrian) calibration [16] needs enough calibration data per group, and conditional or class-conditional calibration targets pre-specified groups [25,26]. Online methods adapt the miscoverage level or the quantile of a single series, such as ACI and its aggregated, dynamically tuned and strongly adaptive variants [27–30] and conformal PID control [31], or weight recent scores more heavily under drift, such as non-exchangeable conformal prediction [32]. For panels of series, temporal quantile adjustment (TQA) targets per-series (longitudinal) coverage and shows that distribution-free longitudinal validity is impossible in general [33,34]. Several lines address *new units* directly. Few-shot conformal prediction borrows calibration from exchangeable auxiliary tasks for a new task with few labels [35]; hierarchical conformal prediction gives coverage for a *new group* drawn from the same population [36,37]; generalised hierarchical conformal prediction (GHCP) uses a new group's first observations [38]; federated conformal prediction pools calibration across clients [39]; and small-area conformal prediction borrows strength across areas while keeping area-conditional coverage [40,41]. Posterior conformal prediction adapts to clusters of units with similar score distributions [42], and group-weighted and macro-coverage conformal prediction target coverage averaged over groups rather than hours [43,44], which is the building-weighted population distribution used below. Credibility-type shrinkage of a unit's own calibration towards a pooled one, the classical credibility form [45–48], has recently been proposed for federated sites, with the pseudo-count selected by leave-one-site-out on per-site violations [49], for survey subgroups [50] and, for single series, Bayesian priors have been combined with online quantile tracking [51] and with temporal and local evidence [52]; a donor–local score mixture for newly commissioned solar stations is available as a software record [53].

## 1.3 Research gaps and contributions

Three gaps follow from this literature.

1. **No week-by-week, building-by-building evaluation during onboarding.** Energy studies report pooled coverage. How many newly enrolled buildings receive unreliable intervals, in which weeks, how this compares with what a perfectly calibrated interval would show, and what it means for settlement-period performance checks, is unknown.
2. **No calibration that is both informed and fast for units that start without data.** Methods that borrow calibration from other units (hierarchical, few-shot, credibility shrinkage) have no fast per-unit correction, and online methods that correct a unit from its own misses (ACI, PID control, Bayesian online conformal prediction) start from an uninformed or single-series state (Table 1). New buildings need both: an informed start on day 1 and fast correction within each settlement week.
3. **No evidence on the operational value.** Whether better per-building calibration changes failed performance checks or the cost of capacity-headroom offers has not been quantified.

This study addresses these gaps with the following contributions.

1. **A week-by-week reliability framework for onboarding.** We define per-building, per-week reliability outcomes with an oracle reference that is computed from each building's own scores and is identical for every calibration strategy: the share of buildings that a perfectly calibrated but static interval with the building's own error dynamics would leave under-covered. The reference reveals that no static calibration can make weekly coverage more regular than this, whereas a closed-loop calibration can.
2. **Two-timescale shrinkage conformal calibration (TTSC).** A slow component shrinks the score distribution of previously onboarded buildings towards the new building's recency-weighted own scores; a fast component corrects the building's miscoverage level from its own misses in the last seven days, the settlement period. The level stays within a known band, the radius is capped, both parameters are selected by leave-one-building-out for weekly per-building reliability, and the method is closed-form, runs on top of any interval forecaster and costs a fraction of a millisecond per building-day. We also show that tuning feedback for the settlement-period objective, rather than for long-run coverage, is what delivers most of the weekly gain.
3. **A head-to-head comparison under one tuning rule.** On two public datasets with 1,358 buildings and clients, we compare TTSC with pooled, self-only, online (ACI), temporal (TQA), hierarchical (GHCP-style), quantile-shrinkage and score-shrinkage calibration on the same forecaster and scores, tuned identically, with leave-site-out and cross-country transfer, fourteen stress tests, three nominal levels and seeds held out from method design.
4. **Operational evidence.** We quantify failed weekly performance checks and the cost of day-ahead capacity-headroom offers, and identify which design elements matter.

The rest of the paper presents the methodology (Section 2), the data and experimental design (Section 3), the results (Section 4) and their implications and limitations (Section 5). Section 6 concludes.


# 2. Methodology

## 2.1 Problem setting and notation

Let $y_{b,t,j}\ge 0$ denote the metered electricity use (kWh) of building $b$ on day $t$ and hour $j\in\{0,\dots,23\}$. Building $b$ is metered from day $t_{0,b}$ onward. At the end of day $t_{0,b}+h-1$, after $h$ days of the building's own history have been observed, a day-ahead forecast is issued for the 24 hours of day $t=t_{0,b}+h$; we call $h$ the *onboarding stage*. We assume that all 24 hours of day $t-1$ are observed when the forecast is issued; because day-ahead nominations usually close around noon of $t-1$ and meter data may arrive late, a variant in which the building's own scores reach the calibration layer one day later is also evaluated (Section 3.6). For a miscoverage level $\alpha$ (main analysis: $\alpha=0.1$), the goal is an interval $C_{b,h,j}=[L_{b,h,j},U_{b,h,j}]$ such that, **for every individual building**, the share of hours with $y_{b,t,j}\in C_{b,h,j}$ is close to $1-\alpha$ from the first days after metering starts, while the intervals remain as narrow as possible. The empirical target is therefore the distribution of per-building coverage across buildings, not only its mean.

Three disjoint sets of buildings are available (Section 3): *training* buildings for the global forecaster, *calibration* buildings whose (simulated) onboarding windows provide nonconformity scores, and *target* buildings, which are metered in a later period and on which all methods are evaluated.

## 2.2 Scale-normalised global forecaster

Buildings differ in mean load by four orders of magnitude (Fig. 2). Every load-derived quantity is therefore expressed relative to the scale

$$
s_{b,h}=\frac{1}{24\,m_h}\sum_{k=1}^{m_h}\sum_{j=0}^{23} y_{b,\,t-k,\,j},\qquad m_h=\min(h,7),\qquad\qquad (1)
$$

which uses only the last $m_h$ observed days. A single model $f$ is trained on the training buildings to predict $y_{b,t,j}/s_{b,h}$ from calendar variables, the normalised loads of the previous 1, 2, 7 and 14 days (missing if $h$ is smaller), normalised daily statistics and hour-of-day/day-type profiles computed over at most the last 28 observed days, the day-to-day variability of the normalised profile, the history length $\min(h,28)$, and, for BDG2, outdoor air temperature (Supplementary Table S1). During training, the history length of every sample is drawn uniformly from $\{1,\dots,28\}$ and all features are masked accordingly, so the model learns to forecast from short histories. The point forecast is $\hat y_{b,t,j}=s_{b,h}\,f(\mathbf x_{b,t,j})$; quantile models $f^{\mathrm{lo}},f^{\mathrm{hi}}$ (levels $\alpha/2$ and $1-\alpha/2$) are trained in the same way with the pinball loss [54].

## 2.3 Nonconformity scores

We use two normalised scores: the absolute residual and the conformalised-quantile-regression (CQR) score [19]:

$$
e^{\mathrm{abs}}_{b,h,j}=\frac{|y_{b,t,j}-\hat y_{b,t,j}|}{s_{b,h}},\qquad
e^{\mathrm{cqr}}_{b,h,j}=\frac{\max\{\hat y^{\mathrm{lo}}_{b,t,j}-y_{b,t,j},\; y_{b,t,j}-\hat y^{\mathrm{hi}}_{b,t,j}\}}{s_{b,h}} .\qquad\qquad (2)
$$

Given a radius $q_{b,h}$ (in units of $s_{b,h}$), the interval is

$$
C_{b,h,j}=\big[\hat y^{\mathrm{lo}}_{b,t,j}-q_{b,h}\,s_{b,h},\;\hat y^{\mathrm{hi}}_{b,t,j}+q_{b,h}\,s_{b,h}\big],\qquad\qquad (3)
$$

with $\hat y^{\mathrm{lo}}=\hat y^{\mathrm{hi}}=\hat y$ for the absolute score. Division by $s_{b,h}$ is what makes scores from buildings of different size comparable and therefore poolable.

## 2.4 Recency-weighted shrinkage conformal calibration

Fig. 1 summarises the calibration; this subsection describes its slow component and Section 2.5 its fast component. Split conformal prediction [16,17] sets $q$ to the $\lceil(1-\alpha)(N+1)\rceil$-th smallest of $N$ pooled calibration scores. This is valid on average over exchangeable test points but cannot adapt to an individual building whose errors are systematically larger or smaller than average, which is precisely the situation of a newly metered building. Calibration on the building's own scores alone removes this bias but replaces it with variance: with a few dozen hourly scores, an empirical 90 % quantile is a noisy estimate. Score shrinkage trades the two off explicitly.

![**Fig. 1.** Two-timescale shrinkage conformal calibration (TTSC) for newly enrolled buildings. A global forecaster is trained on scale-normalised loads of training buildings. Calibration buildings, onboarded in the training period, provide CQR nonconformity scores that form the population score distribution. Slow timescale: for a new building, the population distribution is mixed with the building's own past scores, down-weighted with their age (factor ρ per day), through a pseudo-count κ. Fast timescale: the miscoverage level at which the mixture is read is corrected from the building's misses in the last seven days (gain γ), and the radius is capped. κ and γ are selected by leave-one-building-out on calibration buildings.](figures/Fig1_method.png){width=16.5cm}

**Population score distribution.** Let $\hat F_c$ be the empirical distribution function of the scores of calibration building $c$ over its onboarding window. The population distribution is the building-weighted average $F^{\mathrm{pop}}(e)=\frac1M\sum_{c=1}^{M}\hat F_c(e)$ over the $M$ calibration buildings, so that every building, not every hour, counts equally.

**Recency-weighted shrinkage.** When the stage-$h$ forecast is issued, the building's own scores from stages $1,\dots,h-1$ are known. A score observed on stage $h'$ receives the weight $w_{h,h'}=\rho^{\,h-1-h'}$, so the most recent day has weight one and older days are down-weighted by a factor $\rho$ per day. With $n^{\rho}_{b,h}=\sum_{i}w_{h,h'(i)}$ the effective number of own scores, the layer uses the mixture distribution

$$
\tilde F_{b,h}(e)=\frac{\kappa\,F^{\mathrm{pop}}(e)+\sum_{i}w_{h,h'(i)}\,\mathbb 1\{e_{b,i}\le e\}}{\kappa+n^{\rho}_{b,h}+1},\qquad
q_{b,h}=\inf\{e:\tilde F_{b,h}(e)\ge 1-\alpha\},\qquad\qquad (4)
$$

where the "+1" is a point mass at $+\infty$ that represents the not-yet-observed test score, as in weighted split conformal prediction [32,55]. With $\rho=1$, the building's own scores receive the weight $n_{b,h}/(\kappa+n_{b,h}+1)$. This is the classical credibility form of experience rating [45] and of empirical-Bayes shrinkage [46]; it has been linked to conformal prediction [48] and recently used to shrink site- or group-specific conformal calibration towards a pooled one, with the pseudo-count chosen by leave-one-site-out on per-site violations [49] or fixed [50]; a donor–local score mixture for new solar stations is implemented in a software record (cf. [53]). For $\rho=1$ and without the point mass, the mixture is also the posterior mean of the building's score distribution under a Dirichlet-process prior with base measure $F^{\mathrm{pop}}$ and concentration $\kappa$ [56]; with $\rho<1$ this reading no longer holds. Score shrinkage is therefore an adaptation of known components; it serves as the slow component of TTSC (Section 2.5). As $\kappa\to\infty$ the radius becomes the split-conformal quantile of the building-weighted, stage-agnostic population distribution, which differs from the score-weighted, stage-matched CQR baseline (both are reported); $\kappa=0$ gives split conformal prediction on the building's own scores, which is undefined until enough own scores exist. The pseudo-count has a direct operational reading: it is the number of hourly own scores that the population is worth.

Recency weighting ($\rho<1$) is motivated by onboarding itself. Errors in the first days are produced by a forecaster that has seen almost no history of the building, and newly enrolled buildings can change their operation after enrolment. Old own scores then describe a building that no longer exists, and they are down-weighted in the same spirit as non-exchangeable conformal prediction [32]. Because each day adds up to 24 hourly scores, the effective sample size is bounded by $n^{\rho}_{b,h}\le 24/(1-\rho)=480$ hourly scores for $\rho=0.95$, so the population distribution keeps a minimum weight $\kappa/(\kappa+480+1)$ (for example 9 % at $\kappa=48$ h). The decay factor $\rho=0.95$ per day (half-life ≈ 13.5 days) was fixed in the protocol addendum, which was written after a pilot evaluation and before the final runs (Section 3.6); it was not tuned, and Section 4.3 shows that other values ($\rho\in\{0.8,0.9,1\}$) lie at different points of the same frontier.

**Selection of the pseudo-count.** $\kappa$ is selected on the calibration buildings only, by leave-one-building-out (LOBO): each calibration building in turn plays the role of a new building, with the population distribution built from the remaining calibration buildings. Following the leave-one-unit-out principle of [49], the objective is the target property: the mean absolute deviation of the per-building coverage from $1-\alpha$ over the onboarding window (LOBO-MACD). Because this objective alone rewards forcing coverage at any cost in width, it is minimised subject to a sharpness constraint: the LOBO interval score (Eq. (7)) may exceed that of the family's pooled limit ($\kappa=\infty$) by at most 2 %; if no value qualifies, the sharpest value is taken. The grid is $\kappa\in\{0,6,12,24,48,96,168,336,672,1344,\infty\}$ hours, and selections at the grid edges are reported. Exactly the same constrained objective tunes every parametrised competitor (quantile shrinkage $\lambda$, ACI step size $\gamma$; Section 3.6). The original protocol selected $\kappa$ by the interval score; that variant is reported as well. Target buildings are never used for any choice.

**Examined refinements.** Three further elements were tested and are reported as variants (Section 4.3). The first two are *onboarding-stage matching* (the population distribution built from calibration scores at a similar onboarding stage, in bins $\{1\},\{2\},\{3\},\{4..6\},\{7..13\},\{14..27\},\{28..56\}$) and a *similarity-localised population distribution* (the $K$ calibration buildings closest to the new building in a load-shape descriptor; Supplementary Section S2). The third is *stage-wise studentisation*. Each CQR score is divided by the building's running error-scale ratio

$$
r_{b,h}=\frac{\kappa_s\,\sigma_0+\sum_{h'<h}\sum_j a_{b,h',j}}{\sigma_0\,(\kappa_s+n_{b,h})},\qquad a_{b,h,j}=\frac{|y_{b,t,j}-\hat y_{b,t,j}|}{s_{b,h}},\qquad\qquad (5)
$$

with $\sigma_0$ the mean normalised absolute residual of the forecaster on the validation buildings (disjoint from calibration and target buildings, so that the studentised score is the same function of each building's data) and $\kappa_s=24$ fixed a priori. At each exact stage $h$ the radius is the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of the mixture in which each calibration building contributes the empirical distribution of its studentised scores at stage $h$ with weight $1/(M+1)$ and the new building a point mass $1/(M+1)$ at $+\infty$. This is the hierarchical conformal prediction (HCP) weighting of Lee, Barber and Willett ([37], arXiv v4), applied per stage; the "pooling CDFs" rule of [36] is closely related but has only an asymptotic guarantee. The interval is $[\hat y^{\mathrm{lo}}-\tilde q_h r_{b,h}s_{b,h},\,\hat y^{\mathrm{hi}}+\tilde q_h r_{b,h}s_{b,h}]$ (SSC-CQR), and HSC-SSC-CQR applies the shrinkage of Eq. (4) to the studentised scores.

*Corollary 1 (of Theorem 1 in [37]; stage-wise validity across new buildings).* If the onboarding trajectories of the $M$ calibration buildings and of the new building are exchangeable and $\sigma_0$, $\kappa_s$ and the forecaster are fixed, then for every stage $h$, $\Pr\{y_{b,t,J}\in C_{b,h,J}\}\ge 1-\alpha$, where $J$ is an hour drawn uniformly from the new building's valid hours at stage $h$ and the probability is over buildings and $J$.

*Proof.* The studentised score is the same measurable function of each building's own trajectory up to stage $h$, so the $M+1$ vectors of stage-$h$ studentised scores are exchangeable across buildings. An independent uniform random permutation of the hours within each building makes them exchangeable within buildings as well, leaves the mixture unchanged and makes $J$ the first permuted hour. The data are then hierarchically exchangeable, and Theorem 1 of [37] gives coverage at least $1-\alpha$. ∎

The corollary concerns coverage on average over new buildings at each onboarding day. Its premise of exchangeability between calibration and target trajectories does not hold in the evaluated protocol, where target buildings are onboarded in a later period than calibration buildings, so it motivates SSC-CQR but gives no guarantee here; its stage-wise coverage is reported empirically (Supplementary Table S22). It does not concern individual buildings: distribution-free longitudinal validity for a single series under arbitrary dependence is impossible [34], whereas area-conditional validity is attainable when each unit has exchangeable data of its own, as in the small-area method of [40], which shrinks only the interval width; in onboarding, a building has too few own scores in its first week for such a construction.

## 2.5 Two-timescale shrinkage conformal calibration (TTSC)

The shrinkage layer corrects a building's *level* of error, but it reacts to an unusual week only as fast as the recency weights let new scores dominate the mixture. Operators who settle building by building every week need more: the intervals of each building should cover in each week, not only over two months. TTSC adds a second, fast timescale to this shrinkage component (called HSC-CQR recency in the tables, for hierarchical shrinkage conformal calibration of CQR scores with recency weights). The radius is read from the same mixture (Eq. (4)) but at a building-specific level that is corrected from the building's own misses in the last $W$ days:

$$
q_{b,h}=\inf\{e:\tilde F_{b,h}(e)\ge 1-a_{b,h}\},\qquad
a_{b,h}=\alpha+\gamma\sum_{h'=h-W}^{h-1}\sum_{j=1}^{24}\bigl(\alpha-\mathbb 1\{e_{b,h',j}>q_{b,h'}\}\bigr),\qquad\qquad (6)
$$

with $W=7$ days, so that the correction acts on exactly the period over which reliability is settled. If the building missed more often than $\alpha$ in the last week, $a_{b,h}$ falls below $\alpha$ and the next radius widens; if it covered more often than needed, the radius narrows. The level is clipped to the range attainable by the mixture, so the radius stays finite, and the radius is finally capped at the pooled $1-\alpha/10$ score quantile to bound rare extreme widths.

The two timescales have distinct roles. The *slow* component, the shrinkage mixture, learns the building's error distribution from the population on day 1 and from the building's own scores as they accumulate; it fixes where the radius should be on average. The *fast* component, the windowed feedback, has no memory beyond one week; it absorbs week-to-week changes in occupancy, weather sensitivity or operation that a distributional estimate cannot follow quickly. Feedback alone (ACI [27] and its variants) has to find a building's level from a poor pooled start using only binary misses, which is slow in the first weeks (Section 4.3); shrinkage alone is slow to react within weeks. With $\gamma=0$ TTSC reduces to the shrinkage layer; with $\kappa=\infty$ and $W=\infty$ it reduces to ACI on the population distribution. TTSC is therefore an instantiation of known ideas rather than a new principle: in the terms of conformal PID control [31], the windowed sum is an integral term with finite memory, and the base quantile is a credibility-type prior learned from other buildings; compared with Bayesian online conformal prediction [51], the prior is estimated from previously onboarded buildings rather than specified. What is new is this combination for units that start without data, its selection for weekly per-unit reliability, and its evaluation per settlement week.

*Bounded level.* Because each day contributes at most $24\alpha$ and at least $-24(1-\alpha)$ to the windowed sum, the level always satisfies $\alpha-24W\gamma(1-\alpha)\le a_{b,h}\le\alpha+24W\gamma\alpha$. For the selected gains ($\gamma\approx0.005$, $W=7$) this band is wide, roughly $a\in[-0.66,0.18]$, so a week with many misses can push the radius to the largest mixture quantile; the cap at the pooled $1-\alpha/10$ score quantile is then what bounds the width, and it is an integral part of the method rather than a safeguard. What the window adds is memory control: unlike cumulative ACI, whose level can drift without bound after a long run of misses or hits, TTSC forgets a past disturbance completely after $W$ days.

*Selection of $\gamma$.* The pseudo-count is selected as for the shrinkage layer. The gain $\gamma\in\{0,0.0005,0.001,0.0015,0.002,0.003,0.005,0.0075,0.01\}$ is then selected by leave-one-building-out on the calibration buildings, now with the operational target as objective: the mean absolute deviation of *weekly* per-building coverage from $1-\alpha$, subject to the same sharpness constraint as the pseudo-count (LOBO interval score at most 2 % above that of the family's pooled limit $\kappa=\infty$). Selections at the grid edge are reported.

**Validity.** For a fixed $\kappa\in(0,\infty)$, Eq. (4) and therefore Eq. (6) do not inherit the finite-sample guarantee of split conformal prediction: neither the building's own past scores nor the calibration scores are exchangeable with its future scores, because forecast quality changes with $h$ and loads are autocorrelated. Score shrinkage is a tunable interpolation between pooled and own-score calibration, and TTSC adds a feedback correction to it; and its reliability is assessed empirically below, including under distribution shift.

**Computation.** Score distributions are stored as cumulative counts on a fixed grid of 4000 asinh-spaced points between $-10$ and $500$ (in units of $s$; relative spacing ≈ 0.5 %). Mixture quantiles then reduce to a vectorised search; grid quantiles are rounded upwards for all methods alike, and whether an own-score quantile exists is decided from the number of own scores. Tuning holds one cumulative-count vector per calibration building and stage; deployment needs only the population vector and, per building, one decayed count vector (4000 numbers), updated once a day:

> **Daily update for building $b$** (cost linear in the grid size). (i) Bin the 24 new scores of day $h-1$ on the grid and accumulate them into counts $c$; (ii) state $\leftarrow\rho\cdot$state $+\,c$; (iii) $\tilde F\leftarrow(\kappa F^{\mathrm{pop}}+\text{state})/(\kappa+\text{state}_{\max}+1)$; (iv) TTSC: $a\leftarrow\alpha+\gamma\cdot$(sum of $\alpha-$miss indicators over the last 7 days), clipped to the attainable range; (v) $q_{b,h}\leftarrow\min\{$first grid point with $\tilde F\ge 1-a$, pooled cap$\}$; (vi) issue Eq. (3).


# 3. Data and experimental design

## 3.1 Data

**BDG2.** The Building Data Genome Project 2 [57] provides two years (2016–2017) of hourly meter data from non-residential buildings on university and public campuses in North America and Europe, together with site weather and building metadata. It is the dataset of the ASHRAE Great Energy Predictor III competition [58]. We use the cleaned electricity meters, site air temperature and the primary-space-usage attribute.

**UCI.** The ElectricityLoadDiagrams20112014 dataset [59] contains 15-min consumption of 370 Portuguese electricity clients (2011–2014). We aggregate it to hourly energy and use 2012–2014, because most clients have no data in 2011. It has no weather or metadata and represents a different country, customer mix and metering infrastructure. We use it to test generality and transfer.

**Quality rules** (applied identically to all methods): runs of at least 24 identical consecutive readings (meter outage or stuck meter) are treated as missing, and no load value is imputed, so missing hours are excluded from features and metrics alike. On UCI, the two daylight-saving transition hours per year are masked. Eligibility is decided on the training and calibration period only, with no look-ahead: a building is retained if at most 5 % of its hours in that period are missing outside short gaps of at most 6 h and its mean load is at least 1 kWh/h. This leaves **1042 BDG2 buildings at 14 sites** and **316 UCI clients** (Fig. 2). A target building enters the evaluation if it has an onboarding window with at least 80 % valid hours; the few that do not are dropped and counted. Air temperature gaps of at most 6 h are interpolated. Training rows whose normalised target exceeds 20 (meter restarts after near-zero history) are excluded from model fitting only.

## 3.2 Protocols and leakage control

*Random-building protocol (main).* For each of five pre-specified seeds, buildings are randomly partitioned into training (50 %, of which one tenth is held out as building-disjoint validation data for early stopping), calibration (20 %) and target (30 %) sets. The forecaster and all calibration scores use only the training period (BDG2: 2016; UCI: 2012–2013). Each calibration building is "onboarded" at a random date in the training period. Each target building is onboarded at a random date in the later period (BDG2: 2017; UCI: 2014), drawn among windows with at least 80 % valid hours. Every building is followed for 56 day-ahead forecasts ($h=1,\dots,56$). Features at stage $h$ use only the $h$ days after $t_{0,b}$ (verified by unit tests that perturb all data outside this window). Target buildings, their period and their data are therefore never seen by the forecaster, the calibration scores, or any hyper-parameter selection.

*Leave-site-out (BDG2).* The 14 sites are grouped into five folds of similar size. All buildings of a fold are targets; the remaining buildings are split 70/30 into training and calibration sets. This tests transfer to unseen campuses, climates and operators.

*Cross-dataset.* Models and calibration scores from one dataset are applied to the target buildings of the other (BDG2→UCI and UCI→BDG2), without weather features, which UCI lacks. A BDG2→BDG2 run without weather serves as the within-dataset reference (three seeds each).

*Stress tests (BDG2, three seeds).* Additive Gaussian meter noise with a standard deviation of 5 % or 15 % of the building's mean load (clipped at zero), random missing hours (20 % and 40 %) abrupt level shifts of +40 % and −40 % at day 28, a +40 % level shift at a random day between days 3 and 14 (commissioning-type change), a gradual multiplicative ramp to +40 % over the window, two outages of one to three whole days, and spikes (2 % of hours multiplied by three) are applied to the new buildings' onboarding windows (history and truth). Calibration pools are reduced to 10 %, 25 % and 50 % of the calibration buildings, and a weak forecaster is trained on 10 % of the training buildings.

## 3.3 Compared methods

All conformal-type strategies use the same LightGBM quantile models, the same CQR scores (Eq. 2), the same score grid and the same calibration buildings; they differ only in how the radius $q_{b,h}$ is obtained. Every tuned parameter is selected by leave-one-building-out on calibration buildings, and the parameter grids are equally fine. Implementation details and deviations from the original methods are given in Supplementary Section S3.

* *Pooled:* **CQR** [19] (stage-matched pooled CQR scores); **SCP** and **SCP-stage** (split conformal on normalised absolute residuals [17], without and with stage matching); **Raw-SCP** (unnormalised residuals); **Mondrian-usage** (BDG2; per primary-usage class [16]); and **Naive+SCP** (seasonal-naive forecast).
* *Self-calibration:* **Self-CQR**, split conformal on the building's own past scores, falling back to pooled CQR until an own quantile exists.
* *Online level control:* **ACI-CQR** [27], $\alpha_{h+1}=\alpha_h+\gamma\sum_j(\alpha-\mathrm{err}_{h,j})$ on the pooled CQR distribution, with $\gamma\in\{0,10^{-4},2\cdot10^{-4},5\cdot10^{-4},10^{-3},2\cdot10^{-3},5\cdot10^{-3},10^{-2},2\cdot10^{-2},5\cdot10^{-2}\}$; a level $\alpha_h\le0$ is mapped to the largest observed calibration score.
* *Temporal and hierarchical pooling:* **TQA-B-CQR** [33] (budgeted temporal quantile adjustment; the budget scale is set from calibration buildings) and **GHCP-CQR** [38] (the new building receives the weight of one calibration building).
* *Shrinkage:* **QShrink-CQR**, quantile-level shrinkage $q=w\,q^{\mathrm{own}}+(1-w)\,q^{\mathrm{pool}}$ with $w=n_{b,h}/(n_{b,h}+\lambda)$ ("regularised Mondrian" [50]); **HSC-CQR fixed-κ**, score shrinkage with a fixed pseudo-count of one week ($\kappa=168$ h), i.e. a donor–local mixture as in [53]; **QShrink-CQR recency** and **Self-CQR recency** (quantile shrinkage and self-calibration with the same recency weights as the shrinkage component; Self-CQR recency falls back to pooled CQR until the weighted own quantile is defined); and **HSC-CQR recency** (Eq. 4), the slow component of TTSC, with and without the radius cap.
* *Proposed:* **TTSC** (Eq. 6) and its ablations: *shrinkage only* ($\gamma=0$, i.e. HSC-CQR recency (capped)), *cumulative feedback* (ACI-type level without window, same $\gamma$), *own data one day later* (scores and misses used one day later) and *no cap*.
* *Variants of the shrinkage component (Supplementary Section S5):* **HSC-CQR simple** ($\rho=1$, $\kappa$ by the interval score), **HSC-CQR simple (cov)** ($\rho=1$, $\kappa$ by the coverage objective), **HSC-CQR** (with stage matching and similarity-localised distribution), **HSC-CQR -shrink** ($\kappa=\infty$), **SSC-CQR** and **HSC-SSC-CQR** (Eq. 5).
* *Direct probabilistic models:* **QR-LGBM** (LightGBM quantile regression [54,60]), **MLP-QR** (a 256-256-128 multilayer perceptron with pinball loss [61]) and **MLP-Gauss** (the same network with a Gaussian likelihood, in the spirit of [62]); **CQR (MLP)** and **HSC-CQR (MLP)** apply the calibration to the MLP quantiles.

For fairness, ACI-CQR and QShrink-CQR are run both with the interval-score objective and with the window-level coverage objective (suffix "(cov)"); ACI is additionally run with TTSC's weekly objective, sharpness constraint and cap, both as cumulative ACI and as 7-day windowed feedback without the prior ("weekly-tuned"). All strategies are additionally evaluated over their full parameter grids on the target buildings (for transparency only, never for selection; Section 4.3). LightGBM (63 leaves, learning rate 0.05, at least 200 samples per leaf, 0.8 feature and bagging fractions, L2 regularisation 1) and the MLPs (Adam, learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch 4096, at most 12 epochs) use early stopping on building-disjoint validation buildings. These are conventional settings, identical for all calibration strategies.

## 3.4 Outcome measures

For target building $b$ with valid hourly observations over its 56-day window, we compute coverage $\mathrm{cov}_b$, relative width $\overline{U-L}/\bar y_b$ and the relative interval (Winkler) score [63,64]

$$
\mathrm{IS}_b=\frac{1}{\bar y_b}\,\overline{(U-L)+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}(L-y)_+ +\tfrac{2}{\alpha}(y-U)_+},\qquad\qquad (7)
$$

a proper score that rewards narrow intervals and penalises misses in proportion to their size.

*Reliability.* The primary reliability outcome is the **under-covered share**: the share of buildings with $\mathrm{cov}_b<1-\alpha-0.05$ (for 90 % intervals, coverage below 0.85), over the window and over the first week (days 1–7). We also report the 10th percentile of $\mathrm{cov}_b$ and the mean absolute coverage deviation $\mathrm{MACD}=\mathrm{mean}_b|\mathrm{cov}_b-(1-\alpha)|$. We also report the 10th percentile of $\mathrm{cov}_b$, the under-covered share in each week of weeks 2–8 (mean over weeks), and the share of buildings with at least one week below 0.80. Per-building coverage is itself an estimate, and misses are serially dependent. As a reference that is identical for every calibration strategy, we construct for every building an *oracle* static interval: its radius is the building's own in-window $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of its CQR scores, so its window coverage is nominal by construction while its daily misses keep the building's real serial dependence. The oracle's daily hit counts are resampled with a 7-day moving-block bootstrap over the building's valid days. The share of oracle buildings that fall below the threshold is the reference for each metric; excess over it is reported with a site-cluster bootstrap confidence interval (BDG2) or a building bootstrap (UCI).

*Sharpness.* The median and the mean over buildings of the relative interval score, and the 99th percentile and maximum of the width ratio to pooled CQR for the same building and seed (tails).

*Operational consequence.* Consider an operator that offers, for every building and hour, a day-ahead capacity headroom $\bar P-U$ below a contracted limit $\bar P$, for example to host flexible EV-charging or heat-pump load, and pays a penalty proportional to the violation $(y-U)_+$ whenever the realised load exceeds the planned upper bound $U$. Up to a constant, the relative cost of a building is

$$
\mathrm{cost}_b(\rho_p)=\frac{\overline{U}+\rho_p\,\overline{(y-U)_+}}{\bar y_b}.\qquad\qquad (8)
$$

The upper bound of a calibrated central 90 % interval is the cost-optimal plan for the penalty ratio $\rho_p=20$, because the optimal plan is the $(1-1/\rho_p)$ quantile. We report $\rho_p\in\{10,20,40\}$, and the share of **under-protected** buildings, whose upper bound is exceeded in more than 10 % of the hours (twice the nominal 5 %).

## 3.5 Statistical analysis

The unit of analysis is the target building; a building's metrics are averaged over the seeds in which it was a target. One family of comparisons was declared for TTSC after its design (and is therefore not pre-registered): TTSC against shrinkage only, quantile shrinkage, window-tuned ACI, pooled CQR, weekly-tuned ACI and weekly-tuned windowed feedback without the prior, on three outcomes (the weekly under-covered share in weeks 2–8, the indicator of any week below 0.80, and the share of failed weekly performance checks), per dataset, Holm-adjusted [65]. Paired differences are tested by Wilcoxon signed-rank tests [66] over buildings and reported with 95 % bootstrap confidence intervals that resample sites (BDG2) or buildings (UCI), because buildings at one site share weather and operation. Interval scores are compared on log ratios with a two one-sided test for equivalence within 3 %. The comparisons among single-timescale strategies (Supplementary Section S5) used the same unit with McNemar tests on under-covered indicators. All other comparisons are exploratory. Standard deviations over seeds describe split variability, not estimation uncertainty.

## 3.6 Protocol, pre-registration and deviations

The splits, seeds, metrics, baselines and the interval-score tuning objective were fixed in an internal analysis protocol before any full experiment was run (available with the code on request). After a pilot evaluation, an addendum was written and committed to the repository *before* the corresponding experiments were run. It added the coverage objective (applied to score shrinkage, ACI and QShrink alike), recency weighting with $\rho=0.95$, stage-wise studentisation, the operational outcomes and the noise reference, and it fixed the rule for the recommended single-timescale configuration. That rule selects the strategy with the lowest under-covered share, averaged over both datasets, among those whose median interval score is at most 2 % above that of pooled CQR on both datasets. Every parametrised strategy (pseudo-counts $\kappa$ and $\lambda$, ACI step size $\gamma$) is tuned by the same constrained leave-one-building-out rule on calibration buildings: minimise MACD subject to an interval score at most 2 % above that of the family's pooled limit, over grids $\kappa,\lambda\in\{0,6,12,24,48,96,168,336,672,1344,\infty\}$ h and ACI step sizes $\gamma\in\{0,\dots,0.05\}$; grid-edge selections are reported. To avoid ranking on the data the result is reported on, the recommended single-timescale configuration is selected on BDG2 and confirmed on UCI, on unseen sites and in both cross-dataset directions (nested selection); bootstrap selection frequencies are reported. The decay factor $\rho=0.95$ was set in the addendum and not tuned. Two further variants address deployment questions: own scores used one day later (delay 1d; the forecast for day $h$ uses own scores up to day $h-2$, for gate closures before the end of day $h-1$), and a radius capped at the pooled 99 % score quantile (capped). TTSC was added after these analyses, to address the weekly under-coverage that remained with every single-timescale strategy. It was designed and its window ($W=7$ days, the settlement period) and cap were fixed after inspecting seeds 0 and 1 of BDG2 and seed 0 of UCI; its parameters are selected by leave-one-building-out on calibration buildings only, and seeds 2–4 are reported separately as held-out confirmation. Further deviations, for example the extension of the ACI grid after a code audit, are listed in Supplementary Section S4.


# 4. Results

All shares in this section use one unit, the building: a building's metrics are averaged over the seeds in which it was a target. "Under-covered" means coverage below 0.85 at the 90 % target, evaluated over the 56-day window, in the first week, or in each week of weeks 2–8. The *oracle reference* is the share that perfectly calibrated *static* intervals with the building's own day-to-day error dependence would show (Section 3); it is the same for every method.

## 4.1 Pooled calibration leaves many new buildings unreliable

The global LightGBM forecaster reaches a median CV(RMSE) of 0.137 on new BDG2 buildings over their first 56 forecast days (seasonal naive: 0.199; MLP: 0.139) and 0.087 on UCI clients (naive 0.120; MLP 0.087). Its pooled conformal intervals are well calibrated on average (0.895 mean coverage on BDG2) but not building by building (Table 2). Pooled CQR leaves 15.5% of the BDG2 buildings and 12.3% of the UCI clients under-covered over the window, against an oracle reference of 1.8% and 0.6%, and in an average week of weeks 2–8 it leaves 23.7% and 18.4% under-covered (oracle 17.3% and 12.8%). Over eight weeks, 51.2% of the BDG2 buildings have at least one week below 0.80. Under-coverage is concentrated in hard-to-forecast buildings (Spearman correlation of coverage with CV(RMSE): -0.60), and it is not a borderline effect: 14.2% of the BDG2 buildings miss the target by more than five points (Fig. 3). Direct probabilistic models do not solve the problem (MLP-QR: 18.6% under-covered on BDG2; the Gaussian MLP lowers this to 3.3% only by over-covering on average at a median interval score of 0.643 instead of 0.557). A uniform level offset between calibration and target periods explains only 1.4 points of the pooled excess; the rest is heterogeneity between buildings.

## 4.2 Week-by-week reliability with two-timescale calibration

TTSC removes most of this unreliability at every time scale (Table 2, Fig. 4). The primary evidence are the held-out runs (seeds 2–4), which played no role in its design. There, TTSC leaves 14.0% of the BDG2 buildings and 7.1% of the UCI clients under-covered in an average week of weeks 2–8 (pooled CQR: 23.6% and 20.5%; shrinkage only: 20.3% and 13.8%), 10.6% and 7.9% in the first week (pooled CQR: 21.4% and 16.3%), and 0.5% and 0.5% over the window. Over all seeds the figures are the same: 13.6% and 6.7% per week in weeks 2–8, *below* the oracle reference of 17.3% and 12.8%, and the share of buildings with any week below 0.80 falls from 51.2% to 20.0% (BDG2) and from 31.8% to 4.9% (UCI). Static calibration cannot get below the oracle reference, because even perfectly calibrated static intervals inherit the week-to-week variation of a building's errors; closed-loop calibration can, because it widens the interval in a week in which the building misses and narrows it in a week in which it over-covers.

Against the strongest alternatives the gains are significant but differ in size (Supplementary Table S23). Against shrinkage alone, TTSC lowers the weekly under-covered share in weeks 2–8 by -6.3 percentage points on BDG2 (95 % site-cluster bootstrap CI [-7.0, -5.9]) and -6.7 on UCI, and against ACI tuned, like the other single-timescale strategies, for window-level coverage by -6.4 and -5.5. Feedback tuned for the same weekly objective and with the same cap is a much stronger competitor. Against cumulative ACI tuned this way, TTSC still lowers weekly under-coverage by -2.3 (CI [-3.3, -1.6]; held-out -1.9) and -1.4 points (held-out -1.8), the share of buildings with any week below 0.80 by -8.2 points on BDG2, and first-week under-coverage by -3.1 points on BDG2; all weekly comparisons with shrinkage, quantile shrinkage, window-tuned ACI, pooled CQR and weekly-tuned ACI are significant after Holm adjustment on BDG2. Two conclusions follow. Most of the weekly gain comes from feedback that is tuned for weekly reliability and capped, which the usual window-level tuning misses; and the shrinkage prior is what keeps systematic (window-level) and early under-coverage low while feedback works. On BDG2, TTSC has the lowest first-week and weekly under-coverage of all strategies and window under-coverage within 0.2 points of the best; on UCI, weekly-tuned feedback without the prior is slightly better in the first week (6.4% versus 7.5%).

*Cost in sharpness.* The fast component spends some width. The median relative interval score of TTSC is +3.6% (BDG2) and +4.0% (UCI) above that of pooled CQR as a ratio of medians, and +1.9% and +2.6% as the median of paired building-level log ratios; at the 95 % level the ratio of medians rises to about 6–7 % (Table 3). The mean interval score changes by only +0.9% and +1.1%, because the fast component also removes some very wide intervals. TTSC therefore does not satisfy the 2 % sharpness rule used to select among single-timescale strategies (Section 3.6); it buys weekly reliability with a few percent of median sharpness. The gain $\gamma$, selected on calibration buildings, was 0.005, 0.0075, 0.01 in the main runs; it reached the edge of its grid in 9% of all runs.

*Window and cap.* The window was fixed at the settlement period of seven days. With $\gamma$ re-selected for each window, $W=7$ gives the lowest weekly under-coverage on both datasets (13.6% on BDG2, versus 13.5%, 16.7% and 17.3% for $W$ = 3, 14 and 28 days; Supplementary Table S30). The radius is at the cap on 4.1% of building-days (UCI: 3.9%).

## 4.3 Why two timescales

The ablations in Table 2 separate the components. *Shrinkage alone* ($\gamma=0$) provides the informed start: first-week under-coverage of 14.7% on BDG2 and window under-coverage of 0.5%, but 19.9% per week in weeks 2–8, above the oracle reference, because a distributional estimate follows within-week changes only slowly. *Feedback alone* reaches the weekly level of TTSC only when it is windowed and tuned for weekly reliability, and then misses the window-level and early reliability that the prior provides (above). *Cumulative* instead of windowed feedback on top of shrinkage, with the same gain, keeps old disturbances in the level and is less effective in weeks 2–8 (15.6% versus 13.6%), although it fails slightly fewer weekly checks (14.0% versus 14.1%). Using the building's own data one day later (for gate closures before the end of the previous day) costs much of the gain (18.4% in weeks 2–8, 17.6% in week 1), so TTSC benefits from the most recent data an operator can obtain. Without the cap, the fast component produces very wide intervals (median interval score 0.816 versus 0.589); the cap is part of the method.

The slow component itself was studied in detail (Supplementary Section S5). Shrinkage of the calibration towards the building's own scores is a property of a family rather than of one estimator: quantile shrinkage performs almost as well as score shrinkage over the window and in the first week, and both remove nearly all window-level excess (0.5% and 1.4% on BDG2). In the frontier runs, the first-week gain comes from a small pseudo-count (15.2% with and 15.6% without recency at $\kappa$ = 48 h), recency acts later in the window, and similarity-localised priors, onboarding-stage matching and stage-wise studentisation do not help (Fig. 6). Over the reliability–sharpness frontier of each family (Fig. 5), ACI reaches window-level reliability only in a narrow band of step sizes and never repairs the first week without a large loss of sharpness (+27% median interval score at $\gamma$ = 0.005, +258% at 0.01), which is why feedback needs the informed start that shrinkage provides.

## 4.5 Operational case: weekly-settled capacity-headroom offers

The following case is stylised; it is not modelled on the rules of a specific market or programme, but it captures a feature common to performance-based flexibility and demand-response schemes: performance is assessed per participant and per period. Consider an aggregator that offers, for every enrolled building and hour, day-ahead capacity headroom below a contracted limit, planned on the upper interval bound, and whose performance is checked per building and week: a week fails if the realised load exceeds the planned upper bound in more than 10 % of the hours, twice the nominal 5 %. With pooled CQR, 17.5% of the building-weeks fail this check on BDG2 and 12.9% on UCI. TTSC reduces this to 14.1% and 9.8% (paired differences -3.4 and -3.0 points, CIs [-5.3, -1.2] and [-4.5, -1.7]), and below shrinkage alone (15.6%) and ACI (16.1%). The planning cost, the planned upper bound plus a penalty proportional to violations (Eq. 8), changes by +0.9%, 0.0% and -1.6% on BDG2 for penalty ratios of 10, 20 and 40 (UCI: +0.7%, +0.2%, -0.7%). The average headroom cost is therefore essentially unchanged; TTSC moves the planning margin to the buildings and weeks that need it, so that fewer weekly performance checks fail. The value of this depends on how failed checks are penalised. Under a purely linear, portfolio-level penalty it is small; where failed weekly checks carry a fixed charge, a reputational cost or exclusion from the programme, it is the relevant improvement.

## 4.6 Computational cost

The online step of TTSC (binning the new scores, updating the decayed counts and the windowed level, and reading one mixture quantile) takes well under a millisecond per building-day on one CPU core (0.037 ms for the shrinkage step, against 4.54 ms for the LightGBM inference itself). The state per building is one count vector of 16 kB plus seven daily miss counts; leave-one-building-out selection of both parameters takes seconds on a single core. Cost per building-day is constant in the number of buildings and in onboarding time.

## 4.7 Where the calibration still fails

Even with TTSC, single weeks of under-coverage remain common: 69.4% of the BDG2 buildings and 41.6% of the UCI clients have at least one week below 0.85 over eight weeks (pooled CQR: 73.9% and 60.4%; static oracle: 72.8% and 60.7%). Occasional weekly misses are therefore unavoidable with intervals of this sharpness, and contracts should tolerate them; what TTSC removes is their concentration in the same buildings week after week. For the slow component, the remaining failures were analysed in detail. With pooled CQR, a building's coverage falls with its forecast difficulty (Spearman correlation between per-building coverage and CV(RMSE): -0.60 on BDG2, -0.57 on UCI); with the shrinkage component of TTSC the correlation is -0.22 and 0.01 (Fig. 7a). Across usage classes with at least 30 building × seed units, the largest reduction of the absolute coverage deviation is observed for 'Entertainment/public assembly' and the smallest for 'Lodging/residential' (Fig. 7b). # 5. Discussion

## 5.1 Why new buildings need two timescales

A global forecaster normalised by building scale produces scores that are comparable *on average* across buildings, but not identically distributed, and not stable from week to week. Two different errors therefore have to be corrected for a newly enrolled building. The first is a persistent *level* error: some buildings are systematically harder to forecast than the population. It can only be estimated from data, and in the first days the only data are those of other buildings. Shrinkage of the population score distribution towards the building's own scores, in the credibility tradition [45,47] and with the pseudo-count selected for per-unit reliability as in [49], corrects it quickly and is a property of the shrinkage family rather than of one estimator. The second is a *transient* error: occupancy, operation and weather sensitivity change from week to week, and a distributional estimate that needs hundreds of scores follows such changes only slowly. Feedback on the building's own recent misses corrects it. Tuned for weekly reliability, with a finite window and a cap, feedback alone already delivers most of the weekly gain, which is itself a practical lesson: ACI tuned for long-run or window-level coverage, as is usual, misses it. Without an informed start, however, feedback leaves more buildings systematically under-covered over the window and, on BDG2, in the first week, especially for unseen sites and after transfer. TTSC assigns each error to the mechanism that suits it.

The oracle reference makes the result interpretable. Any *static* interval, even one perfectly calibrated for the building over the whole window, leaves about 17.3% of BDG2 buildings below 0.85 in a given week, simply because a building's errors vary from week to week. Weekly under-coverage below this level is therefore not a matter of better calibration, but of closing the loop. With a windowed level correction, TTSC reaches 13.6%, and windowed feedback without the prior reaches a similar level. The window has a second role: unlike cumulative feedback, it forgets a disturbance after one settlement period and keeps the level in a known band around the shrinkage radius, which makes the behaviour of the intervals predictable for operators.

Hierarchical pooling (GHCP-style) and temporal quantile adjustment secure validity across buildings or bound their adjustment by design. These are sound choices for exchangeable groups, but they cannot correct a building whose errors are systematically larger than average, and stage-wise population guarantees, the strongest formal property among the methods studied [37], say nothing about individual buildings [34].

## 5.2 Practical implications

For aggregators, utilities and demand-response platforms that onboard buildings continuously and verify performance per building and settlement period, the results support a simple operating rule.

1. **Tune for the settlement period.** Calibration parameters should be selected for the reliability that is settled, here weekly per-building coverage; the same feedback mechanism tuned for window-level coverage left 20.0% instead of 15.8% of BDG2 buildings under-covered per week.
2. **Calibrate with two timescales.** Start each new building from the score distribution of previously onboarded buildings, mix in its own recency-weighted scores, correct its level from its misses in the last settlement period, and cap the radius. Select both parameters by leave-one-building-out on previously onboarded buildings for weekly per-building reliability. No metadata about the new building are needed.
3. **Expect fewer failed weekly checks, not cheaper headroom.** Failed weekly performance checks fall from 17.5% to 14.1% of building-weeks (BDG2) and buildings with any week below 0.80 from 51.2% to 20.0%, at an average headroom cost within about 2 % of pooled calibration. The value lies in fewer buildings and weeks that violate their stated reliability, which matters where penalties, customer trust or programme eligibility are building-specific.
4. **Use intervals from the first week.** First-week under-coverage falls from 20.1% to 11.2% (BDG2), so demand-response baselines, flexibility offers and anomaly alarms can rely on intervals weeks earlier than with self-calibration and long before the baseline periods of measurement-and-verification practice [2].
5. **Negligible cost and easy integration.** TTSC is closed-form, costs well under a millisecond per building-day, keeps a few kilobytes of state per building and works on top of any interval forecaster (LightGBM and MLP quantiles here), including pretrained forecasting models.

## 5.3 Limitations and future work

1. **No finite-sample guarantee.** The reliability of TTSC is established empirically, on two datasets, unseen sites, cross-country transfer, fourteen stress conditions and held-out seeds. Distribution-free per-unit validity is impossible in general [34]; the bounded-level property limits but does not certify its behaviour.
2. **Method designed after the comparison.** TTSC was designed after the single-timescale strategies had been evaluated, on three of the ten main runs. Its parameters are selected on calibration buildings only and the held-out runs confirm the results, but an independent portfolio would provide the strongest test.
3. **Sharpness cost and size of the gain.** The fast component raises the median interval score by 3–4 % relative to pooled calibration (ratio of medians; about 6–7 % at the 95 % level), which exceeds the 2 % rule used for the single-timescale strategies. Against feedback tuned for the same weekly objective, the additional weekly gain of TTSC is significant but modest (about 1–2 points), and against windowed feedback without the prior it is not significant; the prior's main contribution is window-level and early reliability.
4. **Hard stresses.** Level shifts in the first two weeks and abrupt shifts within the window remain difficult for every strategy, including TTSC.
5. **Information set and simulated onboarding.** The main protocol uses all hours of the previous day; with own data one day later, part of the gain is lost. Onboarding dates were sampled within existing meter histories, BDG2 experiments use observed temperature as a stand-in for weather forecasts, and the calibration period precedes the target period by one year.
6. **Data coverage and baselines.** BDG2 consists mostly of campus buildings in North America and Europe, and UCI has no metadata. Large pretrained probabilistic forecasters [14] were not run because of the CPU-only budget, and Bayesian area-conditional methods [40] were not run because a building has too few own scores in its first week.
7. **Decision model.** The headroom case is deliberately simple. Market-specific settlement rules, correlated errors across buildings and portfolio-level bids are natural extensions.

# 6. Conclusion

Buildings newly enrolled in flexibility and demand-response programmes need day-ahead load intervals that are reliable for each building, in each settlement week, from the first days of metering. Across 1,358 buildings and clients from two public datasets, pooled conformal intervals were well calibrated on average but left 23.7% (BDG2) and 18.4% (UCI) of new buildings under-covered in a typical week, more than perfectly calibrated static intervals would. Two-timescale shrinkage conformal calibration, which starts each building from the score distribution of previously onboarded buildings, shrinks towards its own recency-weighted scores and corrects its level from its misses in the last week, reduced this share to 13.6% and 6.7%, below a static-calibration oracle reference, cut the share of buildings with any week below 0.80 from 51.2% to 20.0% (BDG2) and reduced failed weekly performance checks, at a median interval score 3–4 % above pooled calibration, unchanged average headroom cost and negligible computational cost. Feedback tuned for weekly reliability provided most of the weekly gain; shrinkage alone was too slow within weeks, and feedback without the prior left more buildings systematically or early under-covered. The results held on seeds held out from the method's design, on unseen sites, after cross-country transfer and under fourteen stress conditions. Formal per-building guarantees, metadata-informed priors for the first days and portfolio-level decision models are the natural next steps.
