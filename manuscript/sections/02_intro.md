# Introduction {#sec:intro}

Buildings account for a large share of electricity demand, and their flexible loads are increasingly used for grid services, demand response and on-site renewable integration [@li2021flex]. Most of these applications need more than a point forecast. Demand-response baselines, day-ahead flexibility bids, measurement-and-verification and anomaly alarms all act on a statement of the form "tomorrow's hourly load of *this* building will lie between $L$ and $U$ with probability $1-\alpha$". Probabilistic load forecasting has therefore become a central topic in energy forecasting [@hong2016tutorial; @hong2016gefcom], and recent work, including several studies in this journal, has developed increasingly expressive probabilistic models for loads, renewables and multi-energy systems [@guo2024; @nie2024; @lindberg2023].

A practical difficulty is that interval forecasts are most needed when a building has just been connected to a monitoring, aggregation or demand-response platform — exactly when the least building-specific data exist. Data-driven building forecasting traditionally relies on months to years of history per building [@amasyali2018]. Transfer learning, federated learning and global or pretrained models now provide useful *point* forecasts for buildings with little history [@ribeiro2018; @fan2020; @pinto2022; @gao2023; @ristovska2026], and large pretrained models report zero-shot probabilistic forecasts for unseen buildings [@emami2023]. Whether the *intervals* attached to such forecasts are reliable for each new building, and how quickly they become reliable, has received little attention: a recent cross-building study reports that split-conformal intervals under-cover on average when transferred to unseen buildings and recommends recalibration before deployment [@hao2026], but it evaluates pooled coverage and does not study how a new building's own data should enter the calibration.

Conformal prediction offers distribution-free calibration of intervals around any forecaster [@vovk2005; @lei2018; @angelopoulos2023] and is increasingly used in energy forecasting [@suresh2025; @moradi2025]. Its standard guarantee is *marginal*: averaged over exchangeable test points, coverage is at least $1-\alpha$. For a fleet of newly metered buildings, a marginal guarantee is compatible with a situation that operators would consider unacceptable — intervals that are far too narrow for volatile buildings and needlessly wide for regular ones, while the pooled coverage looks perfect. Several families of remedies exist in the statistics and machine-learning literature: group-wise (Mondrian) calibration [@vovk2005]; calibration on the unit's own past errors; online adjustment of the miscoverage level, such as adaptive conformal inference [@gibbs2021; @zaffran2022] and temporal quantile adjustment for panels of time series [@lin2022tqa]; hierarchical conformal prediction for new groups with few observations [@dunn2023; @lee2023hier; @mallick2026]; and empirical-Bayes or credibility-type *shrinkage* of a unit's calibration towards a pooled one, which has recently been proposed for sites in federated medical imaging [@shahid2026], for survey subgroups [@rafe2026], as a Bayesian regularisation in online conformal prediction [@zhang2024bayes], and as a donor–local mixture for newly commissioned solar-irradiance stations [@jeon2026]. These methods have been developed under different assumptions and evaluated on different problems; which of them makes day-ahead load intervals reliable for each newly metered building, from which day onwards and at what cost in sharpness, is not known.

This paper answers that question for building loads. Our contributions are:

1. **Problem and evaluation protocol.** We formalise *per-building interval reliability during onboarding* — coverage of each new building over its first weeks, the dispersion of coverage across buildings, the share of clearly under-covered buildings and the evolution with the number of days since metering started — and evaluate it under a pre-registered, strictly building- and period-disjoint protocol on two public datasets with {{n_bdg2}} buildings and {{n_uci}} clients, including leave-site-out and cross-country transfer and stress tests.
2. **A head-to-head comparison of calibration strategies.** Under one protocol, forecaster and score, we compare pooled split conformal and CQR, Mondrian calibration, self-only calibration, adaptive conformal inference, temporal quantile adjustment (TQA-B), a GHCP-style hierarchical pooling, quantile-level shrinkage ("regularised Mondrian"), and pseudo-count shrinkage of the score distribution — together with direct quantile regression and deep probabilistic baselines.
3. **Design evidence for the shrinkage layer.** We study a closed-form pseudo-count shrinkage of the score distribution — referred to as hierarchical shrinkage conformal (HSC) calibration — whose pseudo-count is selected by leave-one-building-out on calibration buildings, and we test whether two building-specific refinements (a similarity-localised population prior and onboarding-stage matching) add value. We find that the shrinkage itself is the active ingredient and that the refinements are unnecessary, which yields a one-parameter layer that can be placed on top of any interval forecaster.

All code, data instructions and machine-generated results are released.
