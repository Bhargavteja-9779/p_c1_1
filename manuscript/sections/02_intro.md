# Introduction {#sec:intro}

Buildings account for a large share of electricity demand, and their flexible loads are increasingly used for grid services, demand response and on-site renewable integration [@li2021flex]. Most of these applications need more than a point forecast. Demand-response baselines, day-ahead flexibility bids, measurement-and-verification and anomaly alarms all act on a statement of the form "tomorrow's hourly load of *this* building will lie between $L$ and $U$ with probability $1-\alpha$". Probabilistic load forecasting has therefore become a central topic in energy forecasting [@hong2016tutorial; @hong2016gefcom]. Recent work has developed increasingly expressive probabilistic models for loads, renewables and multi-energy systems, including several studies in this journal [@guo2024; @nie2024; @lindberg2023].

A practical difficulty is that interval forecasts are most needed when a building has just been connected to a monitoring, aggregation or demand-response platform, and that is exactly when the least building-specific data exist. Data-driven building forecasting has long relied on months to years of history per building [@amasyali2018]. Transfer learning and global models now produce useful *point* forecasts for buildings with little or no history [@ribeiro2018; @fan2020; @pinto2022; @gao2023], and large pretrained models report zero-shot probabilistic forecasts for unseen buildings [@emami2023]. Whether the *intervals* attached to such forecasts are reliable for each new building, and how quickly they become reliable, has, to our knowledge, not been examined.

Conformal prediction offers distribution-free calibration of intervals around any forecaster [@vovk2005; @lei2018; @angelopoulos2023] and is increasingly used for load forecasting [@suresh2025]. Its standard guarantee is *marginal*: averaged over exchangeable test points, coverage is at least $1-\alpha$. For a fleet of newly metered buildings, a marginal guarantee is compatible with a situation that operators would consider unacceptable. Intervals can be far too narrow for volatile buildings and needlessly wide for regular ones, while the pooled coverage still looks perfect. Two simple fixes have opposite weaknesses. Calibrating only on the building's own past errors cannot be done until enough errors exist, and is noisy for weeks after that. Calibrating on the population of other buildings ignores what is learned about the building as its data accumulate. Online corrections such as adaptive conformal inference [@gibbs2021; @zaffran2022] target long-run coverage of a single long series and react slowly from a poor starting point. Statistical work on hierarchical, clustered and few-shot conformal prediction [@dunn2023; @lee2023hier; @ding2023; @fisch2021] shows that borrowing strength across groups can sharpen group-level calibration. It has not been developed for, or evaluated on, the sequential onboarding of new time series in which the forecast quality itself improves as history grows.

This paper addresses that gap with the following contributions:

1. **Problem and evaluation protocol.** We formalise *per-building reliability during onboarding*: coverage, its dispersion across buildings, and its evolution with the number of days since metering started. We evaluate it under a strictly building- and period-disjoint protocol on two public datasets with {{n_bdg2}} and {{n_uci}} buildings or clients, including leave-site-out and cross-country transfer.
2. **Method.** We propose *hierarchical shrinkage conformal* (HSC) calibration, a model-agnostic layer that (i) matches calibration scores to the onboarding stage, (ii) builds a population prior from calibration buildings whose first days look similar, and (iii) shrinks from that prior toward the building's own accumulating scores with a pseudo-count $\kappa$. The update has the closed form of a Dirichlet-process posterior and recovers population and self-only split conformal prediction as limits. $\kappa$ and the neighbourhood size are selected by leave-one-building-out on calibration buildings only.
3. **Evidence.** In pre-registered experiments with five partition seeds, twelve interval methods are compared: classical, conformal, adaptive-conformal, quantile-regression and deep probabilistic baselines. The evaluation includes paired per-building statistical tests, a component ablation, stress tests (meter noise, missing data, abrupt load changes, small calibration pools, a weak forecaster), generalisation to unseen sites and another country, sensitivity, cost, and error analysis. All code, data instructions and machine-generated results are released.
