# Introduction {#sec:intro}

Buildings account for a large share of electricity demand, and their flexible loads are increasingly used for demand response, grid services and on-site renewable integration [@li2021flex]. Most of these applications need more than a point forecast. Demand-response baselines, day-ahead flexibility commitments, measurement-and-verification and anomaly alarms act on statements of the form "tomorrow's hourly load of *this* building will lie between $L$ and $U$ with probability $1-\alpha$". If the upper bound is too low for a particular building, a capacity reservation sized by it fails repeatedly, whatever the average over the portfolio. Probabilistic load forecasting has accordingly become a central topic in energy forecasting [@hong2016tutorial; @hong2016gefcom], and recent work in this journal has developed increasingly expressive probabilistic models [@guo2024; @nie2024; @lindberg2023].

Interval forecasts are needed most when a building has just been connected to a monitoring, aggregation or demand-response platform — exactly when the least building-specific data exist. Transfer learning, federated learning and global or pretrained models now give useful point forecasts for buildings with little history [@ribeiro2018; @fan2020; @pinto2022; @ristovska2026; @emami2023]. Whether the *intervals* attached to them are reliable for each new building, and from which day, has received little attention: a recent cross-building study found that split-conformal intervals under-cover unseen buildings on average and recommended recalibration [@hao2026], but it evaluated pooled coverage and did not ask how a new building's own errors should enter the calibration.

Conformal prediction calibrates intervals around any forecaster [@vovk2005; @lei2018; @romano2019]. Its standard guarantee is *marginal*: averaged over exchangeable test points, coverage is at least $1-\alpha$. For a fleet of newly metered buildings, this is compatible with intervals that are far too narrow for volatile buildings and needlessly wide for regular ones while pooled coverage looks perfect. Several families of remedies exist — calibration on the unit's own past errors, online adjustment of the miscoverage level [@gibbs2021; @lin2022tqa], hierarchical conformal prediction for new groups [@dunn2023; @mallick2026], and credibility-type shrinkage of a unit's calibration towards a pooled one [@shahid2026; @rafe2026; @jeon2026] — but they were developed under different assumptions and evaluated on different problems. Which of them makes day-ahead load intervals reliable for each newly metered building, from which day, at what cost in sharpness and how it should be tuned is not known.

This paper answers these questions for building loads. Our contributions are:

1. **Per-building reliability over time since metering.** We apply per-unit coverage evaluation [@lin2022tqa; @shahid2026] to building onboarding — the distribution of coverage across new buildings, the share of clearly under-covered buildings, its evolution week by week since metering started and its operational meaning for capacity reservations — on {{n_bdg2_plus_uci}} buildings and clients from two countries, with every site held out in turn, cross-country transfer and stress tests.
2. **A head-to-head comparison of calibration strategies** under one forecaster, score and protocol: pooled CQR, self-only calibration, ACI, temporal quantile adjustment, GHCP-style hierarchical pooling, quantile-level shrinkage and pseudo-count shrinkage of the score distribution (PCS), together with direct quantile and neural baselines.
3. **Evidence on how the shrinkage should be tuned and designed.** The usual interval-score tuning is nearly flat and does not deliver per-building reliability; a reliability-aware leave-one-building-out rule does, for PCS and quantile shrinkage alike, without loss of sharpness, whereas online adaptation reaches similar reliability only with much wider intervals. Similarity-localised priors and onboarding-stage matching — the refinements of our own pre-registered design — add nothing, leaving a one-parameter layer that can be placed on top of any interval forecaster.

We also report what does not work: no strategy repairs under-coverage in the first week after metering starts. Code, data instructions and machine-generated results are released.
