# Introduction {#sec:intro}

## Background and motivation

Buildings account for a large share of electricity demand, and their flexible loads are increasingly enrolled in demand response, flexibility markets and on-site renewable integration [@li2021flex]. These applications act on day-ahead statements of the form "tomorrow's hourly load of *this* building will lie between $L$ and $U$ with probability $1-\alpha$". Demand-response baselines and their settlement, day-ahead capacity and flexibility offers, measurement and verification [@ashrae14] and anomaly alarms all depend on such intervals. Probabilistic load forecasting has therefore become a central topic in energy forecasting [@hong2016tutorial; @hong2016gefcom], and recent studies in this journal have developed expressive probabilistic models for loads, renewables and multi-energy systems [@guo2024; @nie2024; @lindberg2023].

Intervals are needed most when a building has just been connected to a monitoring, aggregation or demand-response platform, which is exactly when the least building-specific data exist. Aggregators onboard buildings continuously, and programme performance is checked per building and per settlement period: a building whose upper bound is exceeded far more often than stated in a given week exposes the operator to penalties, failed performance checks or false alarms for that building and that week, however well the intervals perform on average over the portfolio and the season. Three properties are therefore required at once. The intervals must be reliable *building by building*, *week by week*, and *from the first days* after metering starts, without months of building-specific history. This study asks how close day-ahead load intervals can come to these requirements, and how.

## Related work

*Forecasting with little building-specific data.* Data-driven building forecasting traditionally needs months to years of history per building [@amasyali2018]. Transfer learning, federated learning and global or pretrained models now provide useful point forecasts for buildings with little history [@ribeiro2018; @fan2020; @pinto2022; @gao2023; @ristovska2026], and large pretrained models report zero-shot probabilistic forecasts for unseen buildings [@emami2023]. A recent cross-building study found that split-conformal intervals under-cover unseen buildings on average and recommended recalibration before deployment [@hao2026]. These studies evaluate point accuracy or *pooled* interval metrics; whether the intervals are reliable for *each* new building, and from which day on, has not been examined.

*Conformal calibration in energy forecasting.* Conformal prediction gives distribution-free intervals around any forecaster [@vovk2005; @lei2018; @angelopoulos2023], with conformalised quantile regression (CQR) as a strong default [@romano2019]. It is increasingly used for loads, prices and renewables, for example in day-ahead and intraday power markets [@kath2021], with adaptive conformal inference (ACI) [@suresh2025], local online conformal prediction for power-load intervals [@shi2026] and context-aware calibration for renewable forecasts [@moradi2025]. Its guarantee is *marginal*: averaged over exchangeable test points. For a fleet of new buildings, a marginal guarantee is compatible with intervals that are much too narrow for volatile buildings and too wide for regular ones, while pooled coverage looks perfect. An early comparison of interval methods across load profiles already observed that reliability differs between profiles [@almeida2015].

*Unit-level and temporal calibration.* The statistics literature offers several remedies, each developed under different assumptions. Group-wise (Mondrian) calibration [@vovk2005] needs enough calibration data per group, and conditional or class-conditional calibration targets pre-specified groups [@gibbs2025cond; @ding2023]. Online methods adapt the miscoverage level or the quantile of a single series, such as ACI and its aggregated, dynamically tuned and strongly adaptive variants [@gibbs2021; @zaffran2022; @gibbs2024dtaci; @bhatnagar2023saocp] and conformal PID control [@angelopoulos2023pid], or weight recent scores more heavily under drift, such as non-exchangeable conformal prediction [@barber2023]. For panels of series, temporal quantile adjustment (TQA) targets per-series (longitudinal) coverage and shows that distribution-free longitudinal validity is impossible in general [@lin2022tqa; @lin2022cptd]. Several lines address *new units* directly. Few-shot conformal prediction borrows calibration from exchangeable auxiliary tasks for a new task with few labels [@fisch2021]; hierarchical conformal prediction gives coverage for a *new group* drawn from the same population [@dunn2023; @lee2023hier]; generalised hierarchical conformal prediction (GHCP) uses a new group's first observations [@mallick2026]; federated conformal prediction pools calibration across clients [@lu2023fed]; and small-area conformal prediction borrows strength across areas while keeping area-conditional coverage [@bersson2024; @hoff2023]. Posterior conformal prediction adapts to clusters of units with similar score distributions [@zhang2024post], and group-weighted and macro-coverage conformal prediction target coverage averaged over groups rather than hours [@bhattacharyya2024; @bhattacharyya2026], which is the building-weighted population distribution used below. Credibility-type shrinkage of a unit's own calibration towards a pooled one, the classical credibility form [@buhlmann1967; @efron1973; @buhlmann2005; @hong2023cred], has recently been proposed for federated sites, with the pseudo-count selected by leave-one-site-out on per-site violations [@shahid2026], for survey subgroups [@rafe2026] and, for single series, Bayesian priors have been combined with online quantile tracking [@zhang2024bayes] and with temporal and local evidence [@fang2026]; a donor–local score mixture for newly commissioned solar stations is available as a software record [@jeon2026].

## Research gaps and contributions

Three gaps follow from this literature.

1. **No week-by-week, building-by-building evaluation during onboarding.** Energy studies report pooled coverage. How many newly enrolled buildings receive unreliable intervals, in which weeks, how this compares with what a perfectly calibrated interval would show, and what it means for settlement-period performance checks, is unknown.
2. **No calibration that is both informed and fast for units that start without data.** Methods that borrow calibration from other units (hierarchical, few-shot, credibility shrinkage) have no fast per-unit correction, and online methods that correct a unit from its own misses (ACI, PID control, Bayesian online conformal prediction) start from an uninformed or single-series state (Table [-@tbl:positioning]). New buildings need both: an informed start on day 1 and fast correction within each settlement week.
3. **No evidence on the operational value.** Whether better per-building calibration changes failed performance checks or the cost of capacity-headroom offers has not been quantified.

This study addresses these gaps with the following contributions.

1. **A week-by-week reliability framework for onboarding.** We define per-building, per-week reliability outcomes with an oracle reference that is computed from each building's own scores and is identical for every calibration strategy: the share of buildings that a perfectly calibrated but static interval with the building's own error dynamics would leave under-covered. The reference reveals that no static calibration can make weekly coverage more regular than this, whereas a closed-loop calibration can.
2. **Two-timescale shrinkage conformal calibration (TTSC).** A slow component shrinks the score distribution of previously onboarded buildings towards the new building's recency-weighted own scores; a fast component corrects the building's miscoverage level from its own misses in the last seven days, the settlement period. The level stays within a known band, the radius is capped, both parameters are selected by leave-one-building-out for weekly per-building reliability, and the method is closed-form, runs on top of any interval forecaster and costs a fraction of a millisecond per building-day. We also show that tuning feedback for the settlement-period objective, rather than for long-run coverage, is what delivers most of the weekly gain.
3. **A head-to-head comparison under one tuning rule.** On two public datasets with {{n_bdg2_plus_uci}} buildings and clients, we compare TTSC with pooled, self-only, online (ACI), temporal (TQA), hierarchical (GHCP-style), quantile-shrinkage and score-shrinkage calibration on the same forecaster and scores, tuned identically, with leave-site-out and cross-country transfer, fourteen stress tests, three nominal levels and seeds held out from method design.
4. **Operational evidence.** We quantify failed weekly performance checks and the cost of day-ahead capacity-headroom offers, and identify which design elements matter.
