# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

## Why pooled calibration fails per building, and why shrinkage helps

A global forecaster normalised by building scale yields scores that are comparable *on average* across buildings, but not identically distributed: some buildings have regular schedules and small errors, others irregular occupancy, plug loads or weather sensitivity that the first days of data cannot reveal. A pooled quantile is a compromise that over-covers the former and under-covers the latter; in our data this leaves a substantial share of new buildings well below the nominal coverage although the pooled coverage looks satisfactory (Tables [-@tbl:mainbdg2]–[-@tbl:mainuci]). Calibration on the building's own errors alone removes this bias but replaces it with variance — with a few dozen hourly scores, the empirical 90 % quantile is a noisy estimate, which is visible in Self-CQR's first-week coverage. Shrinkage trades the two off explicitly, and its pseudo-count κ has a direct operational reading: the number of hourly own scores that the population prior is worth. The leave-one-building-out selections ({{tuned_BDG2_kappa_simple}} h on BDG2 and {{tuned_UCI_kappa_simple}} h on UCI for the simple variant) mean that a building's own errors dominate after about one to two weeks of data.

The comparison with the other building-adaptive strategies clarifies what matters. Mixing *distributions* (HSC) and shrinking *quantiles* (QShrink) behave similarly when κ and λ are tuned in the same way, with an advantage for the distribution mixture in our data; both clearly outperform strategies that adapt the queried level instead of the score distribution (ACI, TQA-B). ACI updates the level through the binary miscoverage signal with a small step size, which is what keeps it stable over long series but makes it slow from a poor starting point; TQA-B bounds its adjustment by design. The GHCP-style pooling secures marginal validity across buildings by never giving the new building more weight than one reference building — a sound choice for exchangeable groups, but one that cannot correct a building whose errors are systematically larger than average. In short, per-building reliability during onboarding requires the building's own errors to gain weight as they accumulate, and the rate at which they gain weight (κ) is the one parameter that needs care.

## What did not help, and what the pseudo-count trades off

Two refinements that we expected to matter did not. Similarity localisation of the prior (nearest calibration buildings by load-shape descriptor) produced no measurable gain. After normalisation by scale and conditioning of the forecaster on history-derived features, the remaining between-building differences in score distributions appear to be driven by events — occupancy changes, equipment faults, schedule irregularities — rather than by load shape or usage type; the usage-based Mondrian baseline failed for the same reason. Stage matching was unnecessary because the forecaster is told how much history it has (history-length feature and masked lags), so its errors at different stages are already on a comparable scale after normalisation.

The choice of κ embodies a trade-off between sharpness and per-building reliability. The leave-one-building-out objective, the interval score, rewards both and selected mostly two weeks of hourly scores; per-building reliability is best at four days to one week, and a pre-specified pseudo-count of one week produced fewer under-covered buildings on BDG2 at a marginally higher interval score (Table [-@tbl:ablation]; on UCI the two were indistinguishable). Operators who weight under-coverage more heavily than sharpness — for example, when intervals protect demand-response baselines — can therefore choose a pseudo-count of about one week rather than the interval-score optimum; the sensitivity analysis (Fig. [-@fig:sens]) shows that any value between about one day and two weeks performs far better than pooled calibration.

## Practical implications

For aggregators, utilities and demand-response platforms that onboard buildings continuously, the results support a simple operating rule: calibrate the intervals of a global forecaster with CQR scores of previously onboarded buildings, and blend each new building's own scores in with a pseudo-count of about one to two weeks of hourly scores. This keeps the share of buildings with clearly unreliable intervals small throughout onboarding and avoids a failure mode that pooled calibration hides — systematically under-protected baselines for the most irregular buildings, which are the ones for which settlements and alarms are most error-prone. The layer costs well under a millisecond per building-day and a few megabytes of memory, works on top of different forecasters (LightGBM and MLP quantiles here) and is therefore compatible with portfolio-scale deployment, including with pretrained forecasting models.

## Limitations {#sec:limitations}

1. **No finite-sample guarantee for intermediate κ.** The shrinkage layer reduces to split conformal prediction only in its limits, and its reliability for $0<\kappa<\infty$ is established empirically. Distribution-free per-series (longitudinal) validity is impossible in general [@lin2022cptd]; GHCP retains a finite-sample marginal guarantee [@mallick2026] but, as our results show, at the price of little per-building adaptation.
2. **Tuning reflects the calibration population.** κ is selected on calibration buildings; if new buildings differ systematically from them, the population prior is of little value. In the UCI→BDG2 transfer the shrinkage still removed most of the under-coverage, but it was no better than self-only calibration (Table [-@tbl:gen]).
3. **Observed weather.** BDG2 experiments use observed temperature as a stand-in for day-ahead weather forecasts, which favours point accuracy for all methods equally; the UCI and cross-dataset experiments use no weather and show the same qualitative findings.
4. **Simulated onboarding.** Onboarding dates were sampled within existing meter histories. Real onboarding can coincide with commissioning problems or behaviour changes after enrolment, which our level-shift stress tests only approximate.
5. **Data coverage.** BDG2 consists mostly of campus buildings in North America and Europe and UCI has no metadata; residential portfolios, industrial sites and other climates remain to be tested.
6. **Abrupt changes and small pools.** Because past own scores remain in the mixture, level shifts during onboarding increase under-coverage for all strategies, although least for the shrinkage strategies (Table [-@tbl:robust]); the remaining failures of HSC-CQR are concentrated in buildings with large within-window level changes (Section [-@sec:errors]). A very small calibration pool (16 buildings) also degrades reliability. Recency weighting of own scores or a combination with online miscoverage control are untested remedies.
7. **Baselines.** Large pretrained probabilistic forecasters [@emami2023] were not run because of the CPU-only budget, and the GHCP and TQA baselines are adaptations of methods designed for other settings (documented in Section [-@sec:setup]).
8. **Per-building coverage is itself an estimate.** With about {{nvalid_BDG2_median}} hourly observations per building, per-building coverage carries sampling noise (MACD floor ≈ {{floor_BDG2_macd}} for independent misses, higher with autocorrelation), so differences between the best strategies are partly within this noise; the paired tests address this at the population level.

# Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

Day-ahead prediction intervals are needed from the first days after a building is metered, and they must be reliable for each building, not just on average. Across {{n_bdg2_plus_uci}} buildings and clients from two public datasets, pooled conformal and quantile-regression intervals left many new buildings substantially under-covered although their average coverage was close to nominal. Among the building-adaptive calibration strategies compared under one protocol — self-only calibration, adaptive conformal inference, temporal quantile adjustment, GHCP-style hierarchical pooling, quantile shrinkage and pseudo-count shrinkage of the score distribution — the latter was the most reliable: applied to conformalised quantile regression, it reduced the share of under-covered new buildings from {{main_BDG2_CQR_under_rate}} to {{main_BDG2_HSCCQR_under_rate}} on BDG2 and from {{main_UCI_CQR_under_rate}} to {{main_UCI_HSCCQR_under_rate}} on UCI with an essentially unchanged interval score, worked on top of two different forecasters, and remained beneficial for unseen sites, under meter noise, missing data, abrupt level changes, reduced calibration pools and a weak forecaster; under transfer from a different population, the benefit came from the building's own scores alone. The active ingredient is the shrinkage itself; similarity weighting and onboarding-stage matching were unnecessary, which leaves a one-parameter layer whose pseudo-count of one to two weeks of hourly scores can be set from operational priorities. Future work should combine the layer with recency weighting or online miscoverage control for abrupt changes, establish finite-sample guarantees for the shrinkage estimator, and test it with pretrained forecasting models and residential portfolios.
