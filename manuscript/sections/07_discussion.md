# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

## Why pooled calibration fails per building, and what makes the layer work

A global forecaster normalised by building scale produces scores that are comparable *on average* across buildings, but not identically distributed. Some buildings have regular schedules and small errors; others have irregular occupancy, plug loads or weather sensitivity that the first days of data cannot reveal. A pooled quantile is a compromise that over-covers the former and under-covers the latter. In our data, pooled CQR coverage falls with forecast difficulty (Spearman correlation with CV(RMSE): {{ea_BDG2_rho_CQR}} on BDG2), whereas with the recency-weighted layer coverage is almost independent of it ({{ea_BDG2_rho_primary}}). Reliability is no longer something that only easy buildings receive.

The results identify three ingredients. The first is shrinkage of the score *distribution* towards the building's own scores. Mixing distributions, rather than quantiles, lets the first own scores shift the whole tail gradually. The second is a pseudo-count selected for the quantity that matters. The interval score is almost flat in $\kappa$ and therefore gives little guidance, whereas per-building coverage on calibration buildings selected {{tuned_BDG2_hsc_cqr_recency}} h (BDG2) and {{tuned_UCI_hsc_cqr_recency}} h (UCI), i.e. one to two days of hourly scores, for the recency-weighted layer. The third is recency weighting. It keeps the population prior active in the background (the effective number of own scores is bounded by $1/(1-\rho)$) and lets the most recent errors dominate the building's own contribution, which helps both in the first week and after abrupt changes. Online level control can match the window-level reliability of this layer at a well-chosen step size, but only in a narrow band of step sizes, and it cannot repair the first week. Its feedback signal, the binary miss indicator, carries too little information when the starting point is poor. Hierarchical pooling (GHCP) and temporal quantile adjustment secure validity across buildings or bound their adjustment by design. These are sound choices for exchangeable groups, but they cannot correct a building whose errors are systematically larger than average.

The stage-wise studentised variant illustrates a distinction that is easy to overlook. Its guarantee of coverage on average over new buildings at every onboarding day (Proposition 1) is the strongest formal property among the methods studied, yet it leaves many individual buildings under-covered. A per-building target needs per-building information, and no population-level guarantee can substitute for it [@lin2022cptd].

## Practical implications

For aggregators, utilities and demand-response platforms that onboard buildings continuously, the results support a simple operating rule: calibrate the intervals of a global forecaster with CQR scores of previously onboarded buildings, mix in each new building's own scores with an exponential forgetting factor of about 0.95 per day, and select the pseudo-count by leave-one-building-out on the per-building coverage of previously onboarded buildings. This rule has three practical consequences.

1. **Reliability from the first weeks.** The share of new buildings with clearly unreliable intervals falls to the level expected from sampling noise from the second week on, and the first-week share falls from {{wk_BDG2_CQR_w1}} to {{wk_BDG2_HSCCQR_recency_w1}} (BDG2) and from {{wk_UCI_CQR_w1}} to {{wk_UCI_HSCCQR_recency_w1}} (UCI). Applications that rely on intervals at enrolment, such as demand-response baselines, flexibility offers and anomaly alarms, can therefore use them weeks earlier than under self-calibration. Measurement-and-verification practice typically relies on long baseline periods [@ashrae14], and the layer provides reliable day-ahead uncertainty well before such baselines exist.
2. **Risk redistribution, not cost reduction.** Average headroom cost is essentially unchanged, but the planning margin is reassigned from regular to volatile buildings, and under-protected buildings, those whose stated reliability is violated systematically, almost disappear. Where contracts, settlement rules or customer trust are building-specific, this is the relevant improvement.
3. **Negligible cost and easy integration.** The layer is closed-form, needs one parameter selected in seconds, adds well under a millisecond per building-day and works on top of different forecasters (LightGBM and MLP quantiles here), including pretrained forecasting models. It needs no metadata about the new building.

## Limitations and future work {#sec:limitations}

1. **No finite-sample guarantee for the recommended layer.** Its reliability is established empirically, across two datasets, unseen sites, cross-country transfer and stress tests. Distribution-free per-building validity is impossible in general [@lin2022cptd], and the stage-wise guarantee of Proposition 1 does not translate into per-building reliability. Combining shrinkage with a formal per-building safeguard is an open problem.
2. **The first week remains the hardest period.** {{wk_BDG2_HSCCQR_recency_w1}} of the BDG2 buildings are still under-covered over their first week. Until own data accumulate, every strategy can only be as reliable as the information available, which suggests using metadata or pretrained-model uncertainty in the population distribution.
3. **Simulated onboarding and observed weather.** Onboarding dates were sampled within existing meter histories, and BDG2 experiments use observed temperature as a stand-in for weather forecasts. Real enrolment can coincide with commissioning problems that our level-shift tests only approximate. The experiments without weather show the same ordering.
4. **Data coverage.** BDG2 consists mostly of campus buildings in North America and Europe, and UCI has no metadata. Residential portfolios, industrial sites, other climates and sub-hourly resolutions remain to be tested.
5. **Baselines.** Large pretrained probabilistic forecasters [@emami2023] were not run because of the CPU-only budget, and the GHCP and TQA baselines are adaptations of methods designed for other settings (Supplementary Section S3).
6. **Fixed decay factor.** The forgetting factor was fixed a priori at 0.95 per day. The frontier analysis shows that 0.8–0.95 perform similarly, but selecting it jointly with the pseudo-count was not studied.
7. **Decision model.** The capacity-headroom case is deliberately simple. Market-specific settlement rules, correlated errors across buildings in a portfolio and the joint calibration of many buildings' intervals for portfolio-level bids are natural extensions.

# Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

Day-ahead load intervals are needed from the first days after a building is metered, and they must be reliable for each building, not only on average. Across {{n_bdg2_plus_uci}} buildings and clients from two public datasets, pooled conformal and quantile-regression intervals left a substantial share of new buildings under-covered although their average coverage was close to nominal. A recency-weighted shrinkage of the nonconformity-score distribution towards each building's own scores, with the pseudo-count selected for per-building coverage, reduced the share of under-covered new buildings from {{main_BDG2_CQR_under_rate}} to {{main_BDG2_HSCCQR_recency_under_rate}} (BDG2) and from {{main_UCI_CQR_under_rate}} to {{main_UCI_HSCCQR_recency_under_rate}} (UCI), within the level expected from sampling noise. It reduced first-week unreliability from {{wk_BDG2_CQR_w1}} to {{wk_BDG2_HSCCQR_recency_w1}} (BDG2) and from {{wk_UCI_CQR_w1}} to {{wk_UCI_HSCCQR_recency_w1}} (UCI), at an interval score within {{rec_HSCCQR_recency_is_ratio_BDG2}} of pooled CQR. The improvement held on unseen sites, after cross-country transfer, under meter noise, missing data, abrupt level changes, small calibration pools and a weak forecaster, and for a neural quantile model. Online level control and quantile shrinkage reach similar window-level reliability only at well-chosen parameters and do not repair the first week. Similarity priors and onboarding-stage matching are unnecessary. A stage-wise population guarantee does not imply per-building reliability. For operators, the layer does not reduce average cost; it removes systematically under-protected buildings at negligible computational cost. Future work should add formal per-building safeguards, use metadata or pretrained-model uncertainty for the first days, and extend the decision analysis to portfolio-level flexibility bids.
