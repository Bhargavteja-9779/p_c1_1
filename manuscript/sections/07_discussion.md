# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

## Why pooled calibration fails per building, and what repairs it

A global forecaster normalised by building scale produces scores that are comparable *on average* across buildings, but not identically distributed. Some buildings have regular schedules and small errors; others have irregular occupancy, plug loads or weather sensitivity that the first days of data cannot reveal. A pooled quantile is a compromise that over-covers the former and under-covers the latter. In our data, pooled CQR coverage falls with forecast difficulty (Spearman correlation with CV(RMSE): {{ea_BDG2_rho_CQR}} on BDG2), much less so with the recommended layer ({{ea_BDG2_rho_primary}}).

The results point to one mechanism and two choices. The mechanism is *shrinkage toward the building's own scores*: every strategy that lets the new building's scores gain weight as they accumulate removes most of the pooled excess over the onboarding window, whether it mixes score distributions or quantiles. We therefore read the result as a property of the shrinkage family, in line with credibility theory [@buhlmann1967; @buhlmann2005], rather than as a new mechanism. The first choice is the *selection objective*. The interval score is almost flat in the pseudo-count and gives little guidance; selecting it for per-building coverage under a sharpness constraint, as [@shahid2026] did for site-level violations, selects small pseudo-counts of one to seven days of hourly scores, and these are what improve the first week. The second choice is *recency weighting*, which keeps early and pre-change scores from dominating the building's own contribution later in the window and under level shifts or drift.

Online level control (ACI) is a different kind of mechanism: it reacts to misses rather than to scores. At 90 % it can match the window-level reliability of shrinkage only in a narrow band of step sizes, and in the first week the binary miss signal carries too little information to correct a poor starting point. At 80 %, where misses are twice as frequent, the feedback is informative enough and ACI was the selected strategy. Hierarchical pooling (GHCP-style) and temporal quantile adjustment secure validity across buildings or bound their adjustment by design. These are sound choices for exchangeable groups, but they cannot correct a building whose errors are systematically larger than average.

The stage-wise studentised variant illustrates a distinction that is easy to overlook. Its coverage guarantee, averaged over new buildings at every onboarding day (a corollary of [@lee2023hier]), is the strongest formal property among the methods studied, yet it leaves many individual buildings under-covered, and the change between calibration and target periods voids even that guarantee here. A per-building target needs per-building information, and no population-level guarantee can substitute for it [@lin2022cptd].

## Practical implications

For aggregators, utilities and demand-response platforms that onboard buildings continuously, the results support a simple operating rule: calibrate the intervals of a global forecaster with CQR scores of previously onboarded buildings, mix in each new building's own scores with a forgetting factor of about 0.95 per day, select the pseudo-count by leave-one-building-out on per-building coverage of previously onboarded buildings under a sharpness constraint, and cap the radius at a high pooled quantile. Three consequences follow.

1. **No systematically unreliable buildings, but no weekly guarantee.** Over the first eight weeks, the share of buildings whose intervals are systematically too narrow falls from {{x_BDG2_CQR_under}} to {{x_BDG2_HSCCQR_recency_capped_under}} (BDG2, capped layer), and first-week unreliability falls to the level of a perfectly calibrated building. Week-to-week coverage of any single building remains noisy, and on BDG2 about one building in five is below 0.85 in a given week, a few points more than an ideal calibration would produce. Applications that settle building by building over weeks, such as demand-response baselines, flexibility offers and anomaly alarms, should therefore budget for weekly fluctuation even with well-calibrated intervals. Measurement-and-verification practice relies on long baseline periods [@ashrae14]; the layer provides usable day-ahead uncertainty well before such baselines exist.
2. **Risk redistribution, not cost reduction.** Average headroom cost is essentially unchanged, but the planning margin is reassigned from regular to volatile buildings, and buildings whose stated reliability is violated systematically become rare. This matters where contracts, settlement rules or customer trust are building-specific; under a purely portfolio-level linear penalty it has no monetary value.
3. **Negligible cost and easy integration.** The layer is closed-form, adds {{time_BDG2_recency_apply_ms}} ms per building-day to a forecast that itself takes {{time_BDG2_lgb_infer_ms}} ms, keeps {{time_BDG2_state_KB}} kB of state per building, needs one parameter selected in seconds, works on top of different forecasters (LightGBM and MLP quantiles here) and needs no metadata about the new building.

## Limitations and future work {#sec:limitations}

1. **No finite-sample guarantee for the recommended layer.** Its reliability is established empirically, across two datasets, unseen sites, cross-country transfer and fourteen stress conditions. Distribution-free per-building validity is impossible in general [@lin2022cptd]. Combining shrinkage with a formal per-building safeguard is an open problem.
2. **Weekly reliability.** On BDG2, weekly under-coverage in weeks 2–8 remains {{x_BDG2_HSCCQR_recency_excess_wk2_8}} points above the oracle reference (CI {{x_BDG2_HSCCQR_recency_excess_wk2_8_ci}}) for the recommended layer and is above it for every strategy studied. Level shifts in the first two weeks and gradual ramps remain hard for all strategies.
3. **Information set and simulated onboarding.** The main protocol uses all hours of the previous day; the one-day-delayed variant shows that window reliability is retained but first-week under-coverage rises by two to three points. Onboarding dates were sampled within existing meter histories, BDG2 experiments use observed temperature as a stand-in for weather forecasts, and the calibration period precedes the target period by one year, which introduces a small uniform coverage offset.
4. **Data coverage.** BDG2 consists mostly of campus buildings in North America and Europe, and UCI has no metadata. Residential portfolios, industrial sites, other climates and sub-hourly resolutions remain to be tested.
5. **Baselines.** Large pretrained probabilistic forecasters [@emami2023] were not run because of the CPU-only budget; the GHCP and TQA baselines are adaptations of methods designed for other settings (Supplementary Section S3), and Bayesian area-conditional methods such as FAB [@bersson2024] were not run because a building has too few own scores in its first week.
6. **Fixed decay factor.** The forgetting factor was fixed at 0.95 per day; the frontier shows that 0.8 is slightly better on BDG2. Selecting it jointly with the pseudo-count was not studied.
7. **Decision model.** The capacity-headroom case is deliberately simple. Building-level contractual penalties, market-specific settlement, correlated errors across buildings and portfolio-level bids are natural extensions.

# Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

Day-ahead load intervals are needed from the first days after a building is metered, and they must be reliable for each building, not only on average. Across {{n_bdg2_plus_uci}} buildings and clients from two public datasets, pooled conformalised quantile regression left {{x_BDG2_CQR_under}} (BDG2) and {{x_UCI_CQR_under}} (UCI) of new buildings systematically under-covered over their first eight weeks, although its mean coverage was close to nominal. Shrinking each building's calibration toward its own accumulating scores, with the pseudo-count selected for per-building coverage under a sharpness constraint, removed nearly all of this excess ({{x_BDG2_HSCCQR_recency_under}} and {{x_UCI_HSCCQR_recency_under}} with the recommended recency-weighted layer) at an equivalent median interval score, and brought first-week reliability to the level of a perfectly calibrated building. Quantile shrinkage performed almost as well; the result is a property of the shrinkage family. Online level control matched it over the window only in a narrow band of step sizes, and not in the first week at 90 %. The gains held on unseen sites, after cross-country transfer, with a delayed information set, for a neural forecaster and under fourteen stress conditions. Two caveats bound the claim: weekly coverage of single buildings remains noisy and, on BDG2, a few points worse than ideal for every strategy, and the recommended layer needs a radius cap to avoid rare very wide intervals. For operators, the layer does not reduce average cost; it removes systematically under-protected buildings at negligible computational cost.
