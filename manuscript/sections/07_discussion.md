# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

## Why pooled calibration fails per building, and why shrinkage helps

A global forecaster normalised by building scale yields scores that are comparable *on average* across buildings, but not identically distributed. Some buildings have regular schedules and small errors, while others have irregular occupancy, plug loads or weather sensitivity that the first days of data cannot reveal. A pooled quantile is a compromise that over-covers the former and under-covers the latter. In our data this compromise leaves between one in six and one in three new buildings well below the nominal coverage, even though the pooled coverage looks satisfactory (Tables [-@tbl:mainbdg2]–[-@tbl:mainuci]). Calibration that uses only the building's own errors removes this bias but replaces it with variance. With a few dozen hourly scores, the empirical 90 % quantile is a noisy estimate, which is visible in Self-CQR's poor first-week coverage. HSC trades the two off explicitly. Its pseudo-count κ is the number of hourly scores that the population prior is worth, and the selected values ({{tuned_BDG2_kappa}} h on BDG2, {{tuned_UCI_kappa}} h on UCI, i.e. roughly one to two weeks) mean that a building's own errors dominate after about two weeks of data. The sensitivity analysis shows that any κ between one day and two weeks performs similarly (Fig. [-@fig:sens]), so the method is not fragile.

ACI pursues a related goal through online feedback on the miscoverage rate. It is a strong competitor here, but it reacts to the building's errors only through their effect on the miscoverage indicator, with a fixed step size. HSC updates the whole score distribution, so its radius moves to the building's own quantile as fast as the data allow. The two adaptation mechanisms are complementary, and combining them (e.g. ACI on top of HSC) is a natural extension that we did not evaluate.

## What did not help

Two components that we expected to matter did not. Similarity localisation of the prior (nearest calibration buildings by load-shape descriptor) produced no measurable gain. A plausible explanation is that, after normalisation by scale and conditioning of the forecaster on the history-derived features, the remaining between-building differences in score distributions are not predictable from load shapes. They reflect events (occupancy changes, equipment faults, schedule irregularities) rather than building type, and the usage-based Mondrian baseline failed for the same reason. Stage matching was also unnecessary: because the forecaster is told how much history it has (history-length feature, masked lags), its errors at different stages are already on comparable scales after normalisation. We report these negative results because they simplify practice. The recommended configuration of HSC is a single pooled prior and one pseudo-count, which can be implemented in a few lines on top of any interval forecaster.

## Practical implications

For aggregators, utilities and demand-response platforms that onboard buildings continuously, the results suggest a simple operating rule. Calibrate prediction intervals of a global forecaster with CQR scores of previously onboarded buildings, and blend each new building's own scores in with a pseudo-count of one to two weeks of hourly scores. This keeps the share of buildings with clearly unreliable intervals small throughout onboarding (Table [-@tbl:mainbdg2]). It also avoids a failure mode that pooled calibration hides: systematically under-protected baselines for the most irregular buildings, which are precisely those for which demand-response settlements and anomaly alarms are most error-prone. The calibration layer costs well under a millisecond per building-day and a few megabytes of memory, so it is compatible with portfolio-scale deployment and with any forecaster, including pretrained foundation models.

## Limitations {#sec:limitations}

1. **No finite-sample guarantee for intermediate κ.** HSC reduces to split conformal prediction only in its limits. Its reliability for $0<\kappa<\infty$ is established empirically, on two datasets and under stress tests, not theoretically. Load autocorrelation also makes even the limiting guarantees approximate.
2. **Observed weather.** BDG2 experiments use observed temperature as a stand-in for day-ahead weather forecasts. This favours point accuracy for all methods equally, but real forecasts would add error. The UCI and cross-dataset experiments, which use no weather, show the same qualitative findings.
3. **Simulated onboarding.** Onboarding dates were sampled within existing meter histories. Real onboarding can coincide with commissioning problems or behaviour changes after enrolment, which our level-shift stress tests only approximate.
4. **Data coverage.** BDG2 consists mostly of campus buildings in North America and Europe, and UCI has no metadata. Residential, industrial and other climates remain to be tested.
5. **Abrupt changes.** Because past own scores remain in the mixture, HSC adapts slowly to level shifts during onboarding (Table [-@tbl:robust]). Recency weighting of own scores, or ACI on top of HSC, are untested remedies.
6. **Baselines.** Large pretrained probabilistic forecasters [@emami2023] were not run, because of the CPU-only computational budget. HSC is a calibration layer and could be applied on top of them, but their native intervals were not compared.
7. **Per-building coverage is itself an estimate.** With about {{nvalid_BDG2_median}} hourly observations per building, per-building coverage carries sampling noise (MACD floor ≈ {{floor_BDG2_macd}} for independent misses, higher with autocorrelation). Differences between the best methods are therefore partly within this noise; the paired tests account for this at the population level.

# Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

Day-ahead prediction intervals are needed from the first days after a building is metered, and they must be reliable for each building, not just on average. Across {{n_bdg2_plus_uci}} buildings and clients from two public datasets, pooled conformal and quantile-regression intervals left many new buildings substantially under-covered, although their average coverage was close to nominal. Hierarchical shrinkage conformal calibration blends a population score distribution with the building's own accumulating scores through a single pseudo-count. Applied to conformalised quantile regression, it cut the share of under-covered new buildings from {{main_BDG2_CQR_under_rate}} to {{main_BDG2_HSCCQR_under_rate}} on BDG2 and from {{main_UCI_CQR_under_rate}} to {{main_UCI_HSCCQR_under_rate}} on UCI, without increasing the interval score. It was more reliable than self-only calibration and adaptive conformal inference applied to the same scores. The improvement persisted for unseen sites, under meter noise and missing data, with small calibration pools and with a weak forecaster, and it was smaller under cross-country transfer and abrupt load changes. The active ingredient is the shrinkage itself; similarity weighting and stage matching were unnecessary. Future work should combine HSC with recency weighting or online miscoverage control for abrupt changes, establish finite-sample guarantees for the shrinkage estimator, and test it with pretrained forecasting models and residential portfolios.
