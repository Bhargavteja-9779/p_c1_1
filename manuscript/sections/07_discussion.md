# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

## Why new buildings need two timescales

A global forecaster normalised by building scale produces scores that are comparable *on average* across buildings, but not identically distributed, and not stable from week to week. Two different errors therefore have to be corrected for a newly enrolled building. The first is a persistent *level* error: some buildings are systematically harder to forecast than the population. It can only be estimated from data, and in the first days the only data are those of other buildings. Shrinkage of the population score distribution towards the building's own scores, in the credibility tradition [@buhlmann1967; @buhlmann2005] and with the pseudo-count selected for per-unit reliability as in [@shahid2026], corrects it quickly and is a property of the shrinkage family rather than of one estimator. The second is a *transient* error: occupancy, operation and weather sensitivity change from week to week, and a distributional estimate that needs hundreds of scores follows such changes only slowly. Feedback on the building's own recent misses corrects it, but feedback alone cannot find the building's level from an uninformed start, because a binary miss is a weak signal. TTSC assigns each error to the mechanism that suits it.

The oracle reference makes the result interpretable. Any *static* interval, even one perfectly calibrated for the building over the whole window, leaves about {{tt_BDG2_ref_week}} of BDG2 buildings below 0.85 in a given week, simply because a building's errors vary from week to week. Weekly under-coverage below this level is therefore not a matter of better calibration, but of closing the loop. With a windowed level correction, TTSC reaches {{tt_BDG2_TTSC_wk_late}}. The window has a second role: unlike cumulative feedback, it forgets a disturbance after one settlement period and keeps the level in a known band around the shrinkage radius, which makes the behaviour of the intervals predictable for operators.

Hierarchical pooling (GHCP-style) and temporal quantile adjustment secure validity across buildings or bound their adjustment by design. These are sound choices for exchangeable groups, but they cannot correct a building whose errors are systematically larger than average, and stage-wise population guarantees, the strongest formal property among the methods studied [@lee2023hier], say nothing about individual buildings [@lin2022cptd].

## Practical implications

For aggregators, utilities and demand-response platforms that onboard buildings continuously and verify performance per building and settlement period, the results support a simple operating rule.

1. **Calibrate with two timescales.** Start each new building from the score distribution of previously onboarded buildings, mix in its own recency-weighted scores, correct its level from its misses in the last settlement period, and cap the radius. Select both parameters by leave-one-building-out on previously onboarded buildings for weekly per-building reliability. No metadata about the new building are needed.
2. **Expect fewer failed weekly checks, not cheaper headroom.** Failed weekly performance checks fall from {{tt_BDG2_CQR_breach_wk}} to {{tt_BDG2_TTSC_breach_wk}} of building-weeks (BDG2) and buildings with any week below 0.80 from {{tt_BDG2_CQR_any80}} to {{tt_BDG2_TTSC_any80}}, at an average headroom cost within about 2 % of pooled calibration. The value lies in fewer buildings and weeks that violate their stated reliability, which matters where penalties, customer trust or programme eligibility are building-specific.
3. **Use intervals from the first week.** First-week under-coverage falls from {{tt_BDG2_CQR_wk1}} to {{tt_BDG2_TTSC_wk1}} (BDG2), so demand-response baselines, flexibility offers and anomaly alarms can rely on intervals weeks earlier than with self-calibration and long before the baseline periods of measurement-and-verification practice [@ashrae14].
4. **Negligible cost and easy integration.** TTSC is closed-form, costs well under a millisecond per building-day, keeps a few kilobytes of state per building and works on top of any interval forecaster (LightGBM and MLP quantiles here), including pretrained forecasting models.

## Limitations and future work {#sec:limitations}

1. **No finite-sample guarantee.** The reliability of TTSC is established empirically, on two datasets, unseen sites, cross-country transfer, fourteen stress conditions and held-out seeds. Distribution-free per-unit validity is impossible in general [@lin2022cptd]; the bounded-level property limits but does not certify its behaviour.
2. **Method designed after the comparison.** TTSC was designed after the single-timescale strategies had been evaluated, on three of the ten main runs. Its parameters are selected on calibration buildings only and the held-out runs confirm the results, but an independent portfolio would provide the strongest test.
3. **Sharpness cost.** The fast component raises the median interval score by about 2–3 % relative to pooled calibration. Where sharpness matters more than weekly reliability, the gain can be lowered; the selection rule makes this trade-off explicit.
4. **Hard stresses.** Level shifts in the first two weeks and abrupt shifts within the window remain difficult for every strategy, including TTSC.
5. **Information set and simulated onboarding.** The main protocol uses all hours of the previous day; with own data one day later, part of the gain is lost. Onboarding dates were sampled within existing meter histories, BDG2 experiments use observed temperature as a stand-in for weather forecasts, and the calibration period precedes the target period by one year.
6. **Data coverage and baselines.** BDG2 consists mostly of campus buildings in North America and Europe, and UCI has no metadata. Large pretrained probabilistic forecasters [@emami2023] were not run because of the CPU-only budget, and Bayesian area-conditional methods [@bersson2024] were not run because a building has too few own scores in its first week.
7. **Decision model.** The headroom case is deliberately simple. Market-specific settlement rules, correlated errors across buildings and portfolio-level bids are natural extensions.

# Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

Buildings newly enrolled in flexibility and demand-response programmes need day-ahead load intervals that are reliable for each building, in each settlement week, from the first days of metering. Across {{n_bdg2_plus_uci}} buildings and clients from two public datasets, pooled conformal intervals were well calibrated on average but left {{tt_BDG2_CQR_wk_late}} (BDG2) and {{tt_UCI_CQR_wk_late}} (UCI) of new buildings under-covered in a typical week, more than perfectly calibrated static intervals would. Two-timescale shrinkage conformal calibration, which starts each building from the score distribution of previously onboarded buildings, shrinks towards its own recency-weighted scores and corrects its level from its misses in the last week, reduced this share to {{tt_BDG2_TTSC_wk_late}} and {{tt_UCI_TTSC_wk_late}}, below the static oracle reference, cut the share of buildings with any week below 0.80 from {{tt_BDG2_CQR_any80}} to {{tt_BDG2_TTSC_any80}} (BDG2) and reduced failed weekly performance checks, at a median interval score about 2–3 % above pooled calibration and negligible computational cost. Neither timescale alone achieved this: shrinkage alone was too slow within weeks and feedback alone too slow at the start. The results held on seeds held out from the method's design, on unseen sites, after cross-country transfer and under fourteen stress conditions. Formal per-building guarantees, metadata-informed priors for the first days and portfolio-level decision models are the natural next steps.
