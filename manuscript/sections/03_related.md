# Related work {#sec:related}

## Probabilistic load forecasting for buildings

Probabilistic load forecasting is commonly approached through quantile regression, parametric likelihood models or ensembles, and is evaluated with the pinball loss, the continuous ranked probability score, or interval coverage and width [@hong2016tutorial; @hong2016gefcom; @gneiting2007]. For individual consumers, quantile neural networks [@wang2019pinball] and global autoregressive likelihood models [@salinas2020] learn across many series. Recent studies in this journal developed theory-guided and adaptive deep models for district loads [@chen2021tgdlf; @gao2023], attentive quantile temporal convolutional networks for multi-energy loads [@guo2024], data-driven city-scale demand models [@wang2021city], and probabilistic forecasting for solar and co-located wind–PV generation [@nie2024; @lindberg2023]. These works assess reliability with *pooled* metrics over a site or a test period, typically for systems with long histories. The *distribution* of interval reliability across many buildings, and its evolution during the first weeks of data, has not been their object.

## Forecasting with little building-specific data

Cold-start building forecasting has mainly been studied as transfer learning. Seasonal and trend adjustment allows models from data-rich buildings to be reused [@ribeiro2018]. Fine-tuning pretrained networks helps when only weeks of target data exist [@fan2020], and a review of 77 studies identifies load prediction as the main application of transfer learning in smart buildings [@pinto2022]. Large-scale pretraining on simulated buildings enables zero-shot forecasting, with probabilistic outputs, for unseen real buildings including BDG2 [@emami2023]. The BDG2 dataset [@miller2020bdg2] and the ASHRAE Great Energy Predictor III competition [@miller2020gepiii] established it as a benchmark for meter-level forecasting. The emphasis of this line of work is point accuracy or aggregate probabilistic scores. Our contribution is complementary: a calibration layer that can sit on top of any of these forecasters, together with an evaluation of per-building interval reliability.

## Conformal prediction for time series and groups

Split conformal prediction [@vovk2005; @papadopoulos2002; @lei2018] and its quantile-regression variant CQR [@romano2019] provide finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability. For time series, exchangeability fails. Adaptive conformal inference [@gibbs2021] and its aggregated variants [@zaffran2022] adjust the miscoverage level online, and ensemble or weighted methods bound the coverage gap under weak dependence or distribution drift [@xu2021; @barber2023; @tibshirani2019]. Adaptive conformal inference has recently been used for short-term load forecasting [@suresh2025]. Conditional or group-level validity is harder. Mondrian (group-wise) conformal prediction [@vovk2005] requires enough calibration data in every group. Conditional calibration over a function class gives group-conditional guarantees for pre-specified groups [@gibbs2025cond]. Hierarchical formulations treat data as groups drawn from a population and provide marginal coverage for a new group [@dunn2023; @lee2023hier]. Clustered conformal prediction pools classes with similar score distributions to obtain approximate class-conditional coverage with few samples per class [@ding2023]. Few-shot conformal prediction meta-learns a quantile predictor from auxiliary tasks [@fisch2021], and Bayesian-bootstrap conformal prediction places Dirichlet weights on calibration scores [@gibson2025]. HSC borrows the idea of partial pooling from these works. It differs in three respects that are dictated by the onboarding problem. First, a new building initially has no scores, so similarity is measured on load-shape descriptors rather than score distributions. Second, the building's own scores arrive sequentially while forecast quality changes with history length, which motivates stage matching and a pseudo-count that converts naturally into "days of own data". Third, the method is a closed-form update tuned by leave-one-building-out, with no meta-training.
