# Related work {#sec:related}

## Probabilistic load forecasting for buildings

Probabilistic load forecasting is commonly approached through quantile regression, parametric likelihood models or ensembles, and evaluated with the pinball loss, the continuous ranked probability score, or interval coverage and width [@hong2016tutorial; @hong2016gefcom; @gneiting2007]. For individual consumers, quantile neural networks [@wang2019pinball] and global autoregressive likelihood models [@salinas2020] learn across many series. Recent studies in this journal developed theory-guided and adaptive deep models for district loads [@chen2021tgdlf; @gao2023], attentive quantile temporal convolutional networks for multi-energy loads [@guo2024], data-driven city-scale demand models [@wang2021city], and probabilistic forecasting for solar and co-located wind–PV generation [@nie2024; @lindberg2023]. Conformal prediction is increasingly used as a model-agnostic calibration layer in this domain, for example with adaptive conformal inference for short-term load [@suresh2025], online conformal prediction for power-load intervals [@shi2026] or context-weighted calibration for renewable forecasts [@moradi2025]. These works assess reliability with *pooled* metrics over a site, a system or a test period, typically for systems with long histories; how reliability is distributed across many individual buildings, and how it evolves during the first weeks of data, has not been their object. An early comparison of interval methods across electricity-load profiles already noted that interval reliability differs between profiles [@almeida2015].

## Forecasting with little building-specific data

Cold-start building forecasting has mainly been studied as transfer learning. Seasonal and trend adjustment allows models from data-rich buildings to be reused [@ribeiro2018], fine-tuning pretrained networks helps when only weeks of target data exist [@fan2020], a review of 77 studies identifies load prediction as the main application of transfer learning in smart buildings [@pinto2022], and federated transfer learning reaches warm-start accuracy after a few weeks of local data [@ristovska2026]. Large-scale pretraining on simulated buildings enables zero-shot forecasting, with probabilistic outputs, for unseen real buildings including BDG2 [@emami2023]. The BDG2 dataset [@miller2020bdg2] and the ASHRAE Great Energy Predictor III competition [@miller2020gepiii] established it as a benchmark for meter-level forecasting. A recent cross-building evaluation found split-conformal intervals to under-cover unseen buildings on average [@hao2026]. The emphasis of this line of work is point accuracy or aggregate interval scores; our study is complementary and concerns the calibration layer and its per-building reliability.

## Conformal prediction across units and over time

Split conformal prediction [@vovk2005; @papadopoulos2002; @lei2018] and conformalised quantile regression (CQR) [@romano2019] provide finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability. For time series, exchangeability fails: adaptive conformal inference (ACI) [@gibbs2021] and its aggregated variants [@zaffran2022] adjust the miscoverage level online, and ensemble or weighted methods bound the coverage gap under weak dependence or distribution drift [@xu2021; @barber2023; @tibshirani2019]. For *panels* of time series, Lin et al. distinguish cross-sectional from longitudinal (per-series) coverage, show that distribution-free longitudinal validity is impossible in general [@lin2022cptd], and propose temporal quantile adjustment (TQA), which queries a series-specific quantile of the cross-sectional score distribution based on the series' own past errors and evaluates the coverage of the least-covered 10 % of series [@lin2022tqa]; residual quantile regression with unit effects targets the same goal [@batra2023]. Group- or unit-level validity is harder still. Mondrian conformal prediction [@vovk2005] requires enough calibration data in every group; conditional calibration over a function class gives guarantees for pre-specified groups [@gibbs2025cond]; clustered conformal prediction pools groups with similar score distributions [@ding2023]; few-shot conformal prediction meta-learns from auxiliary tasks [@fisch2021]; and personalised or federated methods weight other agents' scores [@vejling2026]. For hierarchical data, Dunn et al. [@dunn2023] and Lee et al. [@lee2023hier] obtain marginal coverage for a new group, and generalised hierarchical conformal prediction (GHCP) uses a new group's first few observations together with reference groups while retaining a finite-sample guarantee, by giving the new group the weight of a single reference group [@mallick2026]. Small-area conformal prediction borrows strength through the conformity score instead [@bersson2024], and synthetic or auxiliary scores can augment scarce calibration data with guarantees [@bashari2025].

Closest to the calibration layer studied here are shrinkage estimators that mix a unit's own calibration with a pooled one through a weight $n/(n+n_0)$ — the classical credibility form [@hong2023cred]: risk-curve shrinkage for sites in federated conformal risk control [@shahid2026], a "regularised Mondrian" that shrinks group-wise quantile thresholds towards the global one [@rafe2026], a Dirichlet-process-type prior regularisation of the empirical score distribution in online conformal prediction [@zhang2024bayes; @gibson2025], and a donor–local mixture with a fixed pseudo-count for newly commissioned solar-irradiance stations, released as an analysis-code record together with pooled, distance-weighted and delayed-feedback ACI calibration strategies [@jeon2026]. The pseudo-count mixture we use is therefore not new as a mechanism. What has been missing is evidence on *which* of these strategies makes day-ahead **load** intervals reliable for each newly metered building — a setting with a large, heterogeneous population of units, a forecaster whose accuracy itself improves with the history length, and a need for calibration from the first day. We compare the strategies directly, select the pseudo-count by leave-one-building-out rather than fixing it, test the value of building similarity and onboarding-stage information, and evaluate the full distribution of per-building coverage under stress and transfer.
