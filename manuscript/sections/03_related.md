# Related work {#sec:related}

## Probabilistic forecasting for buildings with little data

Probabilistic load forecasting uses quantile regression, likelihood models or ensembles, evaluated with the pinball loss, proper scores or interval coverage and width [@hong2016tutorial; @hong2016gefcom; @gneiting2007]. Global quantile and likelihood networks learn across many consumers [@wang2019pinball; @salinas2020], and recent studies in this journal developed deep models for district, city-scale and multi-energy loads and probabilistic renewable forecasts [@chen2021tgdlf; @gao2023; @guo2024; @wang2021city; @nie2024; @lindberg2023]. Conformal prediction is increasingly used as a calibration layer for load and renewable forecasts [@suresh2025; @shi2026; @moradi2025]. These studies assess reliability with *pooled* metrics for systems with long histories, although interval reliability is known to differ between load profiles [@almeida2015].

For buildings with little own data, transfer learning reuses models from data-rich buildings [@ribeiro2018; @fan2020; @pinto2022], federated transfer learning reaches warm-start accuracy after a few weeks [@ristovska2026], and transfer with an interval-adjustment step targets intervals under data scarcity [@jin2022]. BuildingsBench pretrains on simulated buildings and reports zero-shot probabilistic forecasts on real buildings, including the two datasets used here [@emami2023]. A recent cross-building study found split-conformal intervals to under-cover unseen buildings on average and recommended recalibration [@hao2026]. This line of work concentrates on point accuracy or aggregate interval scores; we study the calibration layer that any of these forecasters needs and its reliability for each building.

## Conformal calibration across units and over time

Split conformal prediction and CQR give finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability [@vovk2005; @papadopoulos2002; @lei2018; @romano2019]. For time series, adaptive conformal inference (ACI) adjusts the miscoverage level online [@gibbs2021; @zaffran2022], multi-step variants account for the autocorrelation of horizon errors [@wang2024acmcp], and weighted methods bound the coverage gap under drift [@xu2021; @barber2023; @tibshirani2019]. For *panels* of series, longitudinal (per-series) coverage cannot be guaranteed distribution-free in general [@lin2022cptd]; temporal quantile adjustment (TQA) queries a series-specific quantile of the cross-sectional score distribution and evaluates the worst-covered 10 % of series [@lin2022tqa], and unit-level residual models pursue the same goal [@batra2023]. Group-level validity is addressed by Mondrian calibration [@vovk2005], conditional calibration over function classes [@gibbs2025cond], clustered calibration [@ding2023], few-shot and personalised calibration [@fisch2021; @vejling2026], hierarchical conformal prediction for new groups [@dunn2023; @lee2023hier] and its generalisation (GHCP), which uses a new group's first observations with a finite-sample guarantee [@mallick2026], and empirical-Bayes small-area conformal prediction [@bersson2024].

Closest to this study are estimators that mix a unit's own calibration with a pooled one through the credibility weight $n/(n+n_0)$ [@buhlmann1967; @hong2023cred]: shrinkage of per-site risk curves in federated conformal risk control [@shahid2026], a "regularised Mondrian" that shrinks group-wise quantiles to the global one [@rafe2026], Dirichlet-process-type regularisation in online conformal prediction [@zhang2024bayes; @gibson2025], and a donor–local mixture with fixed pseudo-count for newly commissioned solar-irradiance stations, available as an analysis-code record that also implements pooled, distance-weighted and delayed-feedback ACI strategies [@jeon2026]. We found no publication reporting results from that record, so its findings cannot be compared with ours directly. Two prior findings bear on the present question: per-site evaluation shows that average calibration can hide failing sites [@shahid2026], and quantile shrinkage gave no decisive subgroup gain in survey data [@rafe2026].

The pseudo-count mixture is therefore not new, and neither are per-unit coverage metrics. What is missing is evidence on *which* calibration strategy makes day-ahead **load** intervals reliable for each newly metered building — a large, heterogeneous population of units, a forecaster whose accuracy improves with history length, and a need for intervals from the first day — and on how such a strategy should be tuned. We compare the strategies under one protocol, show that the usual interval-score tuning does not deliver per-building reliability and replace it by a reliability-aware rule, test whether building similarity or onboarding stage add information, and evaluate the full distribution of per-building coverage over time since metering, across sites and countries and under stress.
